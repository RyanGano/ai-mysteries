If the Charge Cage was the heart of the building, the company’s story lived everywhere else.

Detective Mara Ellery learned it in fragments as she was walked back toward the administrative wing. No one gave her a formal presentation. No one seemed to see the need. Instead, the company explained itself the way organizations often do—through repetition, through what people assumed didn’t need saying.

They were working on batteries. That much she understood. Not the kind that went into phones or laptops, but something bigger and more ambitious. Energy storage meant to smooth the unevenness of modern life: electric vehicles that didn’t care where the next charger was, homes that could ride out outages without blinking, infrastructure that could absorb strain instead of failing under it.

‘Resilience,’ Priya Shah said, as if the word itself did explanatory work. ‘That’s what investors respond to.’

Ellery nodded. She’d heard the term used often enough to know it usually meant someone else’s problem had been deferred.

The company had grown quickly. Funding rounds stacked close together, each one larger than the last. Success bred urgency. Urgency bred shortcuts, even when no one called them that. The walls were lined with whiteboards filled with timelines that ended in confident arrows pointing toward dates that had already slipped once.

Michael Holloway’s presence lingered everywhere. Not in portraits or plaques, but in language. People said ‘when Mikey decided’ or ‘after Mikey pushed’ as if he were a force of nature rather than a man who had once written code and knocked on doors for money. He was the reason the place existed. He was also, Ellery gathered, the reason it felt perpetually on the edge of something.

‘Testing isn’t the issue,’ Elias said from behind her, eyes still on the monitors. ‘Testing without margins is.’

‘After next week,’ Priya said, almost to herself, ‘things were going to change.’

Ellery looked at her. ‘Change how?’

Priya hesitated, then shrugged it off like it didn’t matter. ‘Mikey called it a reset. New phase. New rules.’

Marcus’s expression didn’t change, but Ellery noticed his fingers tighten once around his forearm.

Elias gave a short, humorless laugh. ‘He called it cleaning house, didn’t he?’

‘Something like that,’ Priya said. ‘He said he was tired of carrying compromises.’

‘You’d have run the next phase,’ Ellery said to Priya.

‘That was the understanding,’ Priya said. ‘Understandings change. He built the place. He’s allowed to change his mind.’ She said it evenly, the way people say things they have practiced being calm about.

Marcus pushed off the wall. Not aggressively — just with the weight of someone who had been holding something at arm’s length for a while.

‘He was different early on,’ Marcus said. ‘First couple of years, he was the last one out every night. Not because anyone expected it. Because he didn’t want to miss anything.’ He looked at the floor. ‘Place like this, you could still feel like you were building something instead of protecting it.’

Priya said nothing.

‘I don’t know exactly when it changed,’ Marcus continued. ‘Somewhere between one funding round and the next, maybe. He got faster. More certain. Started talking about what the work could become instead of what it was.’ A pause. ‘People like that — when they finally get tired of carrying it, they don’t slow down. They turn around.’

He left without being dismissed.

Ellery watched the space where he’d been and thought about the difference between a man who wanted a reckoning and a man who had already decided what the verdict was.

Priya turned. ‘We were still within—‘

‘Within tolerance,’ Elias finished, sharply. ‘That doesn’t mean stable.’

‘We didn’t break protocol,’ Marcus said, from the corridor. He hadn’t raised his voice, but it carried anyway. ‘Access was controlled. Doors were logged. Nobody was where they weren’t supposed to be.’

Renée let out a quiet breath. ‘Except the one person who didn’t usually go inside,’ she said.

The room stilled—not dramatically, just enough.

Ellery looked from one face to the next. ‘Help me understand something,’ she said. ‘Who decides when a test like this runs?’

Priya answered first. ‘I set the schedule.’

‘I validate the parameters,’ Elias said, a beat later.

‘I clear access,’ Marcus added.

‘And I flag risks,’ Renée said. ‘I don’t stop anything. I escalate.’

Ellery nodded slowly. ‘And SAM?’

‘I verify that required conditions are met,’ SAM said. The voice was even, almost reassuring. ‘And monitor system performance during execution.’

‘Does it ever say no?’ Ellery asked.

A pause—brief, but real.

‘I flag conditions that fall outside acceptable thresholds,’ SAM said.

‘And then?’ Ellery pressed.

Priya’s mouth tightened. ‘Then we decide what to do with that information.’

No one contradicted her.

For a moment, it sounded less like a company explaining itself and more like a group of people quietly checking whether they still trusted the same rules they had that morning.

Ellery began to see how the company held together. Not through hierarchy, but through interdependence. Product needed Science to validate claims. Science needed Security to enforce boundaries. Security needed Safety to justify restrictions. Safety needed Product to listen. Each group controlled something essential. None of them could move forward alone.

Michael Holloway sat above that web without quite belonging to it. He didn’t need access to the Charge Cage to exert influence over it. He didn’t need to control the schedule to accelerate it. His authority came from belief—from the shared assumption that if Mikey thought something was ready, it probably was.

The demonstration scheduled for the following week loomed over every conversation. Investors were coming. Not just to watch, but to decide. Funding at that level was less about proof than confidence. You didn’t need perfection. You needed momentum.

‘This run was about reassurance,’ Priya said. ‘About showing ourselves we were ready.’

Ellery thought of the empty enclosure, the suit laid out neatly on steel. ‘And were you?’ she asked.

Priya hesitated, just long enough to notice.

By the time Ellery returned to the operations floor, she understood why this death mattered beyond the loss of a single life. If the system had failed in a way no one could explain, then everything the company promised—every pitch, every projection—rested on an assumption that might not hold.

She looked back toward the glass and wondered how many decisions had been made here because something usually worked.

And how many more depended on it doing so again.
