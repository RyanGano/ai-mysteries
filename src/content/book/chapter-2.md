The facility did not reveal itself all at once. Detective Mara Ellery passed through it in stages, each one marked by a door, a scanner, a pause while someone else confirmed she was allowed to continue.

The lobby felt like a technology company wanted investors to see it: open, light-filled, deliberately unfinished. Concrete floors, exposed ductwork, a wall-length display looping a silent animation of flowing electrons and stylized battery cells. A receptionist sat behind a low desk, hands folded, eyes tired. The badge Ellery was issued was heavier than it needed to be.

Beyond the lobby, the building narrowed. Offices gave way to corridors. Corridors gave way to security doors that required both badge and escort. Each threshold felt like a decision the building was making about her. You can see this. You can see that. You cannot see what’s next.

By the time they reached the operations level, the air had changed. It carried a faint metallic tang, layered with the hum of active systems and the sharper smell of ozone that never quite dissipated. This was where the work happened, not the talking about it.

The Charge Cage sat at the center of it all, enclosed in glass and composite panels, hazard striping marking its perimeter like a warning drawn by someone who did not trust words alone. From here, it looked smaller than it had from the observation gallery above. Not less dangerous—just more specific. A space built for one purpose and nothing else.

Ellery stopped at the yellow line. ‘This is as close as I go,’ she said, more statement than question.

‘That’s close enough,’ the escort agreed.

The suit lay on a stainless steel table just outside the enclosure. Not destroyed. Not torn away. Simply unzipped, unsnapped, and removed, as if there had been time—because there had been nothing left to save. The material was thick, layered, threaded with sensors and mesh that promised protection. One layer failed. Another should have held.

‘It’s rated for this environment,’ someone said, reading from a report. ‘Electrical isolation. Grounding mesh. Automatic presence detection.’

‘And yet,’ Ellery said.

The body had already been removed. No sheet. No outline in chalk. Just absence. A human-shaped void in a place that was never meant to hold a person for long. There were no signs of struggle. No scrape marks. No instinctive attempt to crawl or run. Whatever had happened, it had happened faster than panic.

‘He would have lost consciousness quickly,’ a technician offered. It sounded rehearsed. Comfort disguised as information.

Ellery nodded without agreeing. She’d seen scenes where people fought for their lives until the last possible second. This wasn’t one of them.

She looked up toward the control room, visible through a second wall of glass. Screens were dark or frozen on paused data. Chairs sat askew, pushed back too fast and never returned.

‘This isn’t supposed to be able to happen,’ Ellery said.

No one argued. No one rushed to explain. That silence told her more than a lecture would have.

‘Whatever went wrong,’ she continued, ‘it didn’t look like panic.’

That unsettled her more than evidence of violence ever could have.
