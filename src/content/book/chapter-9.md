Priya Shah treated the interview room like a conference space someone had booked poorly.

She set her tablet on the table, adjusted the chair, smiled as if alignment were still possible. ‘I want to be helpful,’ she said. ‘But we need to be clear about scope.’

Ellery gestured for her to begin.

Priya told a story about momentum.

Investors coming next week. Confidence runs. Slipping timelines that became reputational risks. She framed decisions as responses, not choices.

‘This wasn’t a stunt,’ Priya said. ‘It was validation.’

‘Validation of what?’ Ellery asked.

‘Of dates we’d already given people,’ Priya said. ‘You commit to a future and you build toward it. The commitment comes first. Engineering catches up.’

‘And when a date can’t slip?’

‘Then I mark the run so it can’t,’ Priya said. ‘It tells everyone the decision’s been made above them. It saves a morning of debate.’ She said it as if it were obviously a kindness.

‘And this run?’

A pause, small enough to miss. ‘This run couldn’t slip.’

‘You approved the test,’ Ellery said.

Priya nodded once. ‘I approved the window.’

‘Not the conditions,’ Ellery said.

‘The conditions were already satisfied,’ Priya replied. ‘That’s the point of a readiness review.’

Ellery tilted her head. ‘You saw the readiness report.’

‘Of course.’

‘Including the confidence margins.’

Priya hesitated, then smiled. ‘Margins aren’t red flags. They’re guidance.’

Ellery didn’t return the smile. ‘There was a recommendation to wait.’

Priya exhaled. ‘There was a recommendation to consider waiting if additional signal clarity was required.’

‘And you decided it wasn’t.’

Priya folded her hands. ‘I decided the system was operating within acceptable bounds.’

‘Acceptable to whom?’

‘To the business,’ Priya said. ‘To the investors who were flying in next week. To the engineers who needed proof that we weren’t endlessly rehearsing.’

Ellery leaned forward slightly. ‘You could have delayed.’

‘For what?’ Priya asked. ‘Another week? Another review? Another meeting where nothing changes?’

Ellery waited.

‘Look,’ Priya said. ‘SAM didn’t say no. Science didn’t object formally. Safety logged concerns but didn’t halt. Security cleared access. At some point, someone has to say go.’

‘And that someone was you.’

Priya nodded. ‘That’s what Product is for.’

Ellery watched her carefully.

‘You didn’t override the system,’ Priya said. ‘You trusted it.’

‘Because it had never been wrong before,’ Ellery said.

Priya’s voice softened. ‘Because it had always been right enough.’

She leaned forward. ‘That distinction matters.’

Ellery let it hang.

Priya described Mikey as a force multiplier. Someone who believed delay was its own kind of failure.

‘He trusted the tech,’ she said. ‘That’s why we were here at all.’

‘Who benefits if it worked?’ Ellery asked.

‘Everyone,’ Priya said.

‘And if it didn’t?’

Priya’s smile thinned. ‘No one.’

She gestured outward. Science wanted margin. Safety wanted certainty. Security wanted control.

‘Product wanted proof,’ she said. ‘You can’t ship fear.’

Ellery asked about SAM.

Priya brightened. ‘SAM changed everything. Fewer missed checks. Faster cycles. Fewer human errors.’

‘Did it ever say no?’

Priya hesitated. ‘It flagged things. We evaluated them.’

‘Who decided?’

‘We did,’ Priya said. ‘Collectively.’

After Priya left, Ellery turned to the speaker.

‘SAM. Did Priya Shah’s approval alter system behavior?’

‘Yes,’ SAM replied. ‘Authorization increased execution likelihood.’

‘Not activation.’

‘Correct.’

Ellery underlined the word likelihood twice.
