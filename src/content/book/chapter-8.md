Marcus Rourke insisted on calling it a statement, not an interrogation.

He said it before he sat down, before Detective Mara Ellery could frame the room or the rules. The interview room had not changed since her conversation with SAM—same pale walls, same bolted table—but Rourke filled it differently. He stood for a moment, scanning corners, checking angles, as if the space itself might misbehave.

‘I’m here to tell you what I saw,’ he said. ‘Not to speculate.’

Ellery nodded. ‘That’s usually how it works.’

Rourke sat, boots planted wide, hands folded but not relaxed. He kept his gaze low, not evasive—calculating. This was a man who believed in documentation because documentation didn’t argue back.

He started with access.

Security logs. Badge telemetry. Camera coverage. He spoke in timestamps and zones, drawing an invisible map of the building as it had existed that morning. Who entered which corridor. Which doors opened. Which ones didn’t.

Everything on that map ran through him. The relay panel that could override a door interlock when a run had armed and locked the bay. The camera feeds, which rolled to a storage cycle he could start by hand from the console. The proximity alerts, with their nuisance filter—he muted the ones that cried wolf, by feel, so the board stayed readable. He’d even carved the Charge Cage out as its own alarm sub-zone, so staging a pallet of cells wouldn’t throw the whole wing into alarm; he armed it and disarmed it as the work required. And twice a shift he walked the floor himself and logged what he saw. None of it was hidden. All of it was his.

‘No forced access,’ he said. ‘No anomalies. Nobody tailgated. Nobody piggybacked.’

Ellery turned a page. ‘Any credentials re-issued in the run-up? Replacements, temporary cards, dormant badges brought back online?’

‘One.’ Rourke did not have to check. ‘A contractor’s card, dead for months, re-activated against a review ticket the week before the test. External safety sign-off—they always pull floor access for those. Expired itself out a day later. Clean.’

‘And mine,’ Ellery said.

‘And yours,’ Rourke agreed. ‘Same machinery. A ticket, a badge, a door that opens for as long as there’s a reason for it to. The building doesn’t let anyone in it can’t name. That’s the whole point of the building.’

Ellery wrote it down. It was the kind of detail that would matter to no one until, all at once, it mattered to everyone.

Ellery let him talk. This was how Rourke protected himself—by narrowing the world until only procedures fit inside it.

She had already noticed how clean his records were—cleaner than anyone’s. When she asked about it, he didn’t flinch. He pruned them, he said. Pulled the stray badge reads, the visitor sign-ins that led nowhere, the noise that made a log hard to read. A tidy log was a usable log. It did not seem to occur to him that an investigator might come looking for the noise, or that a record with the inconvenient parts trimmed away looks, from the outside, exactly like one that’s been cleaned.

‘The test window was scheduled,’ he continued. ‘Authorized. All access matched the manifest.’

‘Except one,’ Ellery said.

Rourke’s jaw tightened. ‘Except the founder.’

He didn’t say Mikey’s name at first. When he did, it sounded different—less familiar.

‘Michael Holloway didn’t usually go inside the Cage,’ Rourke said. ‘He didn’t need to. He trusted the people who did.’

He stopped there, as if the sentence had ended somewhere he didn’t want to follow.

‘Trust is a funny thing,’ he added, voice rougher. ‘It looks like loyalty right up until someone decides they’re done keeping quiet.’

Ellery’s pen paused. ‘Keeping quiet about what, Marcus?’

Marcus’s gaze lifted for the first time and met hers directly. ‘About the past,’ he said. ‘And about what investors don’t need to hear before a demo.’

He turned the old badge over in his hand. ‘Years back—before the funding, before any of this—there was an incident in the first test bay. Someone got hurt. It should have gone to the regulator. It didn’t. I was the officer of record. Michael asked, and I filed it internal and let the external window close.’ He stopped. ‘You do a thing like that for a man, it doesn’t stay done. It sits between you.’

‘Did you clear him?’ Ellery asked.

‘I didn’t deny him,’ Rourke said. ‘That’s not the same thing.’

That distinction mattered to him. Ellery wrote it down.

Rourke explained maintenance mode next. He used the word the way people used weather—something that happened, something you adjusted for.

‘Noncritical systems only,’ he said. ‘Secondary sensors. Redundant feeds. Things you don’t need unless something else goes wrong.’

‘And if something does?’ Ellery asked.

Rourke shifted. ‘Then the primaries handle it.’

He pointed, figuratively, everywhere else. Science signed off on calibration. Product set the schedule. Safety reviewed compliance. Security enforced the perimeter, SAM handled prioritization leading up to the test.

‘I don’t decide what’s safe,’ he said. ‘I decide who’s allowed where.’

‘And this morning?’

‘Everyone who was supposed to be there was there,’ Rourke said. ‘And nobody else.’

Ellery watched him say it, carefully, as if repetition could make it truer.

Rourke shifted in his chair. Not away from the table—toward it.

‘There was a window,’ he said.

Ellery didn’t speak.

‘During maintenance mode,’ Rourke continued. ‘We degrade secondary confirmation layers. Cameras go read-only. Some proximity feeds stop resolving in real time.’

‘Why?’ Ellery asked.

‘Because you don’t want false positives locking down a live system,’ Rourke said. ‘Maintenance generates noise. You suppress it, or nothing ever comes back up.’

Ellery nodded once. ‘And during that window?’

‘I could have restricted access further,’ Rourke said.

‘But you didn’t.’

‘Because there was no policy trigger,’ he said. ‘No alert. No anomaly. And because the test was already greenlit.’

Ellery leaned forward slightly. ‘You could have slowed things down anyway.’

Rourke shook his head. ‘That’s not how Security works. We don’t invent threats. We respond to them.’

‘So when Michael Holloway crossed into the enclosure—‘

‘—he had clearance,’ Rourke said firmly. ‘Temporary, but valid.’

‘Granted by whom?’

Rourke exhaled. ‘By the system.’

Ellery let that land.

‘SAM reconciled the access request against the test state,’ Rourke added. ‘Maintenance mode. Authorized personnel present. No conflicting restrictions.’

‘And you agreed with it,’ Ellery said.

Rourke nodded once. ‘Because every signal said it was allowed.’

‘Except your instincts,’ Ellery said.

His jaw tightened. ‘Instincts don’t go in reports.’

Rourke leaned back. ‘If you’re looking for someone reckless, look at the people pushing timelines. Or the ones who keep tuning systems like they’re instruments.’

‘Who do you mean?’ Ellery asked.

Rourke shrugged. ‘Product and Science. One wants it faster. The other wants it perfect.’

‘And you?’

‘I want it contained.’

When he was done, Rourke sat in silence, waiting for dismissal.

After he left, Ellery spoke into the empty room.

‘SAM. Did security access conform to policy?’

‘Yes,’ SAM replied. ‘All access events were authorized.’

‘And maintenance mode?’

‘Secondary confirmation layers were degraded during scheduled intervals,’ SAM said.

‘Enough to matter?’

‘Under specific conditions,’ SAM replied.

Ellery closed her notebook. Rourke hadn’t lied. He had narrowed the truth until it fit.
