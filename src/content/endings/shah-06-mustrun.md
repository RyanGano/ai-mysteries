No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a single email.

'You have a designation,' she said. '"Must-run." It's a flag you can put on a test. It tells everyone—engineering, safety, the floor—that this one happens no matter what. It overrides the normal go/no-go.' She looked at Priya Shah. 'You put it on the 09:30 test at 06:00 that morning. Before anyone else was even in the building.'

'Must-run is a legitimate prioritization tool,' Priya said. 'We have customer demonstrations, board commitments, funding gates. Some tests genuinely cannot slip. The flag exists so the team doesn't waste a morning debating a decision that's already been made above them.'

'A decision already made above them,' Ellery repeated. 'So when the technician found a reason to pause—'

'Did the technician find a reason to pause?'

'Jonah Pike was uneasy about the maintenance window,' Ellery said. 'Renée Calder had a stop-work memo. Both of them, on a normal morning, might have slowed it down. Might have raised a hand. But you'd flagged it must-run. And must-run means the hands stay down.' She paused. 'You didn't override their objections. You made it so their objections never got made. That's more efficient.'

'I removed ambiguity,' Priya said. 'A team that re-litigates every test never ships anything. Sometimes leadership is just refusing to reopen settled questions.'

'The question wasn't settled,' Ellery said. 'It was suppressed. There's a difference, and you know it, because you chose the word "must-run" specifically so it couldn't be reopened.' She leaned forward. 'Every safety system in this building depends on someone being able to say stop. You built a flag whose entire purpose is to make sure no one does.'

The room was quiet.

'The flag didn't kill him,' Priya said. 'A flag is a priority label. It doesn't reach into the bay.'

'No,' Ellery agreed. 'It reaches into the people. It tells the technician his unease doesn't count today. It tells the safety officer her memo is too late before she's even written it. It takes every human who might have stopped this and tells them, in advance, in writing, that stopping isn't available.' She set the email down. 'You didn't disable a machine. You disabled the people.'

Priya said nothing.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that the test carried a must-run designation issued at 06:00. It will note who issued it. It will note that the designation foreclosed the standard go/no-go review.'

She stood.

'You like to say you make the hard calls,' she said. 'But must-run isn't a hard call. It's the opposite. It's the call that makes sure no one else has to make one. You took the decision away from everyone who'd have to live near the consequences, and kept it for yourself, at 06:00, alone, when it was easy.'

She left the email open on the screen. Subject line: MUST-RUN — 09:30. Sent 06:01.
