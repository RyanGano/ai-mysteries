No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a maintenance record from a year ago.

'You damaged a connector,' she said. 'Last spring. On Suit Seven. A pin got bent during a swap—it happens, it's not a crime. The right move is to flag it, replace the housing, log it.' She looked at Jonah Pike. 'You straightened the pin and put it back.'

Jonah went pale.

'I fixed it,' he said. 'It worked. I tested it and it worked fine. A bent pin you can straighten, it's not—people do that. If I'd flagged it they'd have known I bent it, and I was new, and I'd already had two write-ups, and a third one and I'm—' He stopped.

'You'd have been fired,' Ellery said.

'I'd have been fired,' he said.

'So you hid it,' she said. 'And it worked, for a year. The straightened pin carried current just fine for a year of normal runs. And then a year of being bent and straightened and flexed every time the suit moved, and the metal got tired the way bent metal does, and on the morning of the incident, under the big discharge, it failed. At the worst possible moment. The way tired metal does.'

The room was very quiet.

'I didn't know,' Jonah said. 'I swear to God I didn't know it would—I thought it was fixed. It was fixed. For a year it was fixed.'

'I believe you didn't know,' Ellery said. 'I don't think you walked around for a year waiting for it to fail. I think you fixed it, genuinely believed it was fine, and then forgot it had ever happened. That's how we live with the things we hide. We hide them from ourselves too.'

'It could've been anything,' Jonah said, grasping. 'A pin fails, it could've been a manufacturing thing, it could've been—you can't even be sure it was the one I—'

'It was the one you straightened,' Ellery said. 'The metallurgy's clear. The failure was at the rework point. The pin remembered being bent. Metal does that. It carries the old shape forever, no matter how straight you make it look.'

Jonah was crying a little, silently, the way people do when they've been waiting a year to.

'Here's what I keep thinking,' Ellery said. 'A year ago, you had two write-ups and a bent pin and a choice. And the choice wasn't really about the pin. The pin was fine, you were right about that, mostly. The choice was whether to be the kind of person who tells the truth when it's expensive.' She paused. 'You weren't, that day. And the pin waited a year to find out.'

'The report,' she said, 'will note an unrecorded connector repair on Suit Seven, performed approximately one year prior. It will note that the failure originated at the rework site. It will note that the repair was never logged.'

She stood.

'You should have flagged it,' she said. 'They probably wouldn't even have fired you. People bend pins. But you couldn't risk it, and the thing you couldn't risk was small, and it grew.'

She left the year-old record on the table, next to the new one.
