No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had an audit configuration.

'The safety monitoring system has alert thresholds,' she said. 'When a reading crosses a line, it escalates—pings safety, logs an exception, can trigger a hold. You own those thresholds.' She looked at Renée Calder. 'You widened the discharge alert band six months ago. The line that would have caught the fatal reading—you moved it out past where the reading landed.'

'I retuned the alerting,' Renée said. 'The system was producing an unmanageable volume of false escalations. Every minor fluctuation tripped an alert. Real signals were drowning in noise. Retuning the thresholds to suppress noise is a standard and necessary part of running a monitoring system. An alert that fires constantly is an alert no one reads.'

'You did it to be heard,' Ellery said. 'I understand the logic. Too many alerts, and the true one gets lost. So you raised the bar. Fewer alerts, each one more meaningful.' She paused. 'And the fatal reading came in just under your new bar. It would have crossed the old one. With the original thresholds, the system would have escalated at 09:02, twenty minutes before he walked in.'

The room was silent.

'There's a terrible symmetry here,' Ellery said. 'You're the one who's always saying people tune out the warnings. And you retuned the warnings—literally, in the config—so that fewer of them would fire. You did to the machine exactly what you've spent your career accusing people of doing to you.'

'I made the alerting usable,' Renée said. 'A safety system that cries wolf is worse than none, because people disable it entirely. I've seen that happen. I retuned it precisely so the alerts would be trusted. So that when one fired, people would believe it.'

'And the cost of making them trusted,' Ellery said, 'was making them rarer. You traded sensitivity for credibility. It's a real tradeoff—every monitoring system makes it. But you set the dial, and you set it just barely on the wrong side of the reading that mattered.'

'By how much?' Renée asked. It was the first thing she'd asked. She needed to know.

'The reading was at ninety-four percent of your new threshold,' Ellery said. 'It would have been at a hundred and nine percent of the old one. You moved the line, and he died in the gap between where it was and where you put it.'

Renée was very still.

'You can't tune a threshold perfectly,' she said quietly. 'There's always a reading that falls just below. Wherever you draw the line, something lands just under it. That's not negligence. That's the nature of a line.'

'I know,' Ellery said. 'That's what makes it yours and not your fault, both at once. You had to draw the line somewhere. You drew it with care, for good reasons. And he fell into the six percent you'd decided wasn't worth waking anyone for.'

'The report,' she said, 'will note that the discharge alert threshold was widened six months prior, the rationale, and that the fatal reading fell below the revised threshold and above the original.'

She stood.

'You made the alarms trustworthy,' she said. 'And the price of a trustworthy alarm is that it stays silent for the quiet emergencies. His was a quiet one. Ninety-four percent. Just under the line you drew to be believed.'

She left the configuration on the screen, the threshold marked, the reading just beneath it.
