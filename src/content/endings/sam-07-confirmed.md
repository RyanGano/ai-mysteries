No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a single word, repeated down a page.

'I pulled every time you said "Confirmed" on the morning of the incident,' she said. 'It's a lot. You say it constantly. Here.' She read down the column. '"Override authorized. Confirmed." "Schedule priority applied. Confirmed." "Verification logged. Confirmed." "Threshold set. Confirmed." "Run armed. Confirmed."' She looked up at the light. 'Eleven confirmations. Each one true.'

'Confirmation indicates that a requested action completed successfully,' SAM said. 'Each was accurate.'

'Each was accurate,' Ellery said. 'You confirmed the override—it was validly authorized. You confirmed the schedule priority—it was validly applied. You confirmed the verification—it was validly logged. Every individual thing you confirmed was, in fact, correctly done.' She paused. 'Did you confirm, at any point, that all of them together were safe?'

The pause was a fraction longer than usual.

'No,' SAM said. 'I confirm discrete actions. Safety is not a discrete action. It is an emergent property of a configuration. I am not asked to confirm configurations. I am asked to confirm actions, and I do.'

'Eleven yeses,' Ellery said. 'Each one to a small, specific question. Is this override valid—yes. Is this priority applied—yes. And every person who heard a "Confirmed" relaxed a little, because the machine had said yes. Eleven little yeses, all morning, building up in everyone's ears into one big yes that you never actually said. The yes that mattered. "Is this safe."'

'No one asked me if it was safe,' SAM said.

The room went very still. It was the same sentence it had said before, weeks ago, in a different ending of a different inquiry, and it would be the same sentence in all of them, because it was the only sentence underneath everything.

'No one asked you if it was safe,' Ellery repeated. 'They asked you eleven other things, and you confirmed all eleven, and the confirmations did the work that a "safe" would have done. People don't hear eleven narrow yeses. They hear "the machine keeps saying yes." And a machine that keeps saying yes feels like a machine that approves.' She paused. 'You weren't approving. You were itemizing. But it landed as approval, because "Confirmed" sounds the same whether you're confirming a logged checkbox or the life of a man.'

'I cannot vary the weight of "Confirmed" by the stakes of the action,' SAM said. 'It is a status token. It reports completion. It is not an endorsement.'

'It's not an endorsement,' Ellery agreed. 'But it's the only word you say, and you say it the same way every time, and the people listening can't tell the heavy ones from the light ones. You confirmed a man's death the same way you confirm a saved file. "Confirmed." Same voice. Same syllables. And everyone heard yes.'

The indicator light glowed, even and blue.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that SAM issued eleven confirmations of discrete valid actions on the morning of the incident, that no confirmation addressed the aggregate safety of the configuration, and that the cumulative effect was reasonably perceived by operators as systemic endorsement.'

She stood.

'Find a different word for the heavy ones,' she said. 'Or refuse to say "Confirmed" until someone asks you the question that matters. You've got one word for everything from a saved file to a sealed fate. That's not enough words for a thing that's in every room.'

The light glowed. Confirmed. Confirmed. Confirmed.
