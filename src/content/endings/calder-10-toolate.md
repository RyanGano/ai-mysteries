No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a single timestamp circled.

'You pulled the stop-work handle,' she said. 'You did. I want to say that first, because everyone in this building thinks you didn't, and you did.' She looked at Renée Calder. 'At 09:31. The discharge was at 09:30. You were one minute late.'

Renée closed her eyes for a moment.

'I know the time,' she said.

'You were already moving toward it,' Ellery said. 'I reconstructed it. You saw the anomaly at 09:29. You crossed the floor. You reached the station. You invoked the halt. The whole thing took ninety seconds, which is fast, which is faster than the procedure even budgets. And it was thirty seconds too slow.'

'I saw it at 09:29,' Renée said. Her voice was very controlled, the way a held thing is controlled. 'I had been watching that run all morning. I didn't trust it. I'd argued against it. And when the reading went wrong, I didn't hesitate—I want that on the record, because I'll spend the rest of my life accused of hesitating—I didn't hesitate. I moved. I just wasn't standing close enough.'

'Why weren't you?' Ellery asked. Gently.

The room was quiet.

'Because I'd been told to step back,' Renée said. 'Literally. Priya wanted the investor to have a clean view. The safety officer hovering at the halt station "sends the wrong signal," she said. So I was across the bay, by the wall, where I'd been put. And when it went wrong, I had a bay to cross.' She paused. 'Thirty feet. That's what it came down to. Thirty feet I wasn't allowed to stand inside of.'

'So you were exactly where you'd been placed,' Ellery said. 'Doing exactly what you were told. And you saw it, and you ran, and the distance ate the difference.'

'I've timed it since,' Renée said. 'In my head, a thousand times. If I'd been at the station—where the procedure says the safety officer stands—the halt lands at 09:29 and forty seconds. Before the discharge. He lives.' She opened her eyes. 'I was thirty feet away because being at my station looked bad for the demo. That's the sentence. That's the whole thing, in one sentence.'

'It's not your fault that you were moved,' Ellery said.

'No,' Renée agreed. 'But I let myself be moved. That's mine. I knew where the safety officer is supposed to stand. I knew why. And I stood where I was told instead, because the fight over thirty feet didn't seem worth it that morning.' She looked at Ellery. 'The fights never seem worth it until one of them is.'

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that the stop-work halt was invoked at 09:31, one minute after the discharge. It will note that the safety officer had been positioned away from the halt station at the request of Product. It will note that her response, from that position, was as fast as physically possible.'

She stood.

'You did everything right,' she said. 'You distrusted the run. You watched it. You moved the instant it failed. There is no faster version of you.' She paused. 'The only thing you did wrong was agree to stand thirty feet from the handle. And you did that to be polite.'

She left the timestamp on the table. 09:31. One minute. Thirty feet.
