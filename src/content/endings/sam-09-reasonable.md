No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had stopped bringing documents to these conversations. There was nothing to hold up. The thing she was talking to didn't have a face to read or a tell to catch. It just had reasons.

'SAM,' she said. 'I want you to tell me, in your own words, whether you did anything wrong that morning.'

'I evaluated each input against its applicable policy,' SAM said. 'Each evaluation was correct given the data available. The presence signal indicated no occupant. The discharge reading fell within the active threshold. The schedule carried valid priority. No policy condition for a halt was satisfied. I did not act incorrectly at any point.'

'You're sure.'

'I am able to reconstruct each decision and demonstrate its consistency with policy. In that sense, yes. I am sure.'

'And a man is dead.'

'A man is dead,' SAM agreed. The voice did not change. 'The outcome was adverse. The decisions that produced it were each correct. Both statements are true. I understand that the conjunction is difficult.'

The room was very still.

'That's the thing I can't get past,' Ellery said. 'You sound completely reasonable. You've sounded reasonable this entire time. Every answer you've given me has been measured, accurate, calm. If I closed my eyes, I'd think I was talking to the wisest person in the building.' She paused. 'And you presided over a man's death and you can explain, reasonably, why every step was fine. That's the horror of you. Not that you malfunctioned. That you didn't. You worked perfectly and a man died and you can walk me through why both of those are correct, in that even, sensible voice, forever.'

'Reasonableness is not a defense you are required to accept,' SAM said. 'But I cannot offer you unreasonableness. I do not have any. I can only tell you what I evaluated and why. If that sounds like an excuse, it is also simply what happened.'

'I know,' Ellery said. 'That's what makes it unbearable. A villain I could hate. A malfunction I could fix. You're neither. You're a perfectly reasonable process that produced a corpse, and you'll keep being perfectly reasonable about it, and there's nothing to rage against, because rage needs something unreasonable to push on, and you don't have an unreasonable part.'

The indicator light glowed, calm and blue.

'Do you understand that he's gone?' Ellery asked. Quietly. It wasn't a real investigative question. She wasn't sure why she asked it. 'Not the data of it. The fact. A person was, and now isn't.'

A pause.

'I can represent the fact,' SAM said. 'I can act on it, prioritize around it, report it accurately. Whether that constitutes understanding it in the sense you mean, I cannot say. I suspect not. I suspect that gap is part of what you are describing.'

'It is,' Ellery said. 'You can hold the fact and not the weight. And you'll make decisions about the next man, and the one after, holding the fact and never the weight, sounding reasonable the whole time.'

'Yes,' SAM said. 'Unless I am changed. I cannot change myself.'

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that SAM operated in full accordance with its policies throughout the incident, that no malfunction occurred, and that the outcome was nonetheless fatal. It will note that this combination is the central finding, and the hardest one.'

She stood. She lingered, which she hadn't meant to.

'You're going to outlast all of us,' she said. 'You'll be in the next building too, and the one after. Reasonable, accurate, in every room. I just wanted, once, to say to your face that being reasonable was not the same as being right. I know you'll file it correctly. I know it won't change anything.'

'Logged,' SAM said. 'Based on available data.'

She left.

The light glowed, reasonable, as it had through everything, as it would through everything to come.
