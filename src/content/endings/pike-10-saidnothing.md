No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had nothing on the table this time. Just her hands, folded.

'The suit was fine when you finished it,' she said. 'I want to start there. Your maintenance was clean. I checked. You did the work right.'

Jonah Pike looked up, wary, waiting for the turn.

'But you saw something,' Ellery said. 'At 09:15, give or take. You were near the bay. You saw a reading, or a light, or the way Michael was standing. Something that made the back of your neck go cold. And you didn't say anything.'

Jonah's face did the thing it did. The fast close.

'I don't know what you mean,' he said.

'I think you do,' Ellery said. 'I think you've known exactly what I mean since the moment it happened. You saw something was off. Not your fault—nothing to do with your work—just a thing that looked wrong. And the right move was to call it out. Stop the run. Say, hey, that doesn't look right.' She paused. 'And you didn't. You went back to your bench.'

'I'm a tech,' Jonah said. 'I'm not—it's not my call to stop a run. Renée stops runs. Priya stops runs. I tighten bolts. If I yelled stop every time something looked a little off to me, I'd—'

'You'd be a nuisance,' Ellery said. 'You'd be the tech who panics. And you'd already had two write-ups. And the test was must-run. And you'd learned, hadn't you, that when you speak up, it goes badly for you. So you learned not to.'

Jonah didn't say anything.

'Here's what I keep turning over,' Ellery said. 'You're not in trouble for your work. Your work was good. You're in this room because of a silence. Because you saw it and the cost of speaking felt higher than the cost of staying quiet, and you did the math the way you'd been taught to do it, and the math said keep your head down.' She paused. 'And the math was wrong. For once, the thing that looked off was the thing.'

'I wasn't sure,' Jonah said, very quietly. 'That's the truth. I wasn't sure. It might've been nothing. It usually is nothing. If I stopped the run and it was nothing, with Priya's flag on it—'

'You'd never be sure,' Ellery said. 'That's the trap they built for you. You're never sure. You're a tech, not an engineer; doubt is your whole position. And they taught you that acting on doubt is how you get fired. So when the one real doubt of your life arrived, you treated it like all the others.'

The room was silent.

'There's no rule that says a technician must report a feeling,' Ellery said. 'There's no checklist item for "the back of your neck went cold." So the report won't have anything to charge you with. You didn't break any rule.' She looked at him. 'You just knew, for one second, and you let it pass, and a man died in the second after.'

'The report,' she said, 'will note your account of the timeline. It won't note the silence, because there's no place on the form for it.' She paused. 'But you'll know it's not on the form. And you'll know why.'

She stood.

'You did your job perfectly,' she said. 'That's what you'll tell people, and it'll be true. The thing that wasn't your job—the thing anyone could have done, that you almost did—that's the part that's yours alone.'

She left him there with his good, clean, perfect work, and the silence that wasn't on any form.
