No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a sequence of events on the screen.

'The fail-safe,' she said. 'When the system can't confirm a person, it resolves to a safe state. It de-energizes the cells the only way the cells can be de-energized—by completing the discharge into the grounding path. No alarm. No override. The system just does the safe thing.' She looked at Renée Calder. 'You wrote the definition of the safe thing.'

Renée was very still. 'Explain that,' she said. Not defensive. Precise.

'A failsafe doesn't have to prevent harm,' Ellery said. 'You told me that yourself. It has to reduce risk to an acceptable level. So someone had to decide what "safe" meant for the Charge Cage—what the system should do when it isn't sure anyone's there. That definition is a safety artifact. It's yours. Your signature is on it.'

The room was silent.

'The safe state assumes no one is in contact with the suit,' Ellery said. 'It's a safe state for the equipment. When the presence signal dropped out, the system reached for the safe state you'd defined, and the safe state completed the discharge, because that's what's safe when there's no one there.' She paused. 'There was someone there. The definition didn't have a word for him.'

'I wrote that definition years ago,' Renée said quietly. 'For a smaller rig, a different suit. It was carried forward. Revalidated on paper every audit cycle. Nobody ever ran the human-in-suit case against it, because a human isn't supposed to be in the suit during a fault.' Her voice didn't shake; it widened. 'I asked for that case to be analyzed. More than once. It's in the backlog. You can find it.'

'I did find it,' Ellery said. 'You're right. You flagged the exact gap that killed him—a safe state that isn't safe with a person inside—and it was never closed. So here's where we are. You defined the failsafe. You identified the flaw in it. You were overruled. And then the system reached for your definition, by the book, in the one condition you'd warned it was never tested for.' She paused. 'You were proven right in the worst possible way. By your own words, carried forward.'

The room was very quiet.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that the system resolved to its defined safe state in accordance with policy. It will note that the safe-state definition was never validated for a human-in-suit condition. It will note that this gap had been formally raised by you and not addressed.'

She stood.

'There's no fault here that's yours,' she said. 'I want to say that clearly, because you're the kind of person who will take it all anyway. The definition was reasonable when you wrote it. The thing that was broken was the assumption underneath it—and you're the one who'd been trying to get someone to test the assumption. It failed exactly where you said it would.'

Renée didn't answer. She was looking at the sequence on the screen, at the safe state doing precisely what safe had been defined to mean.

'You said it once,' Ellery said at the door. '"The system behaved as designed. That doesn't make it acceptable." You were talking about something else, that day. But you'll be saying it about yourself now.'

She left the sequence on the screen.
