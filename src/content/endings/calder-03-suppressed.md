No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had an audit trail with a deletion in it.

'There was a record,' she said. 'A safety review you signed off on, eight months ago. It approved the test protocol that was running when Michael Holloway died. Your signature certified it as compliant.' She looked at Renée Calder. 'After the incident, that record was deleted. From your account.'

Renée was quiet for a moment. 'That's a serious allegation.'

'It's not an allegation,' Ellery said. 'It's a log entry. The record existed at 09:00. It was gone by 13:00. The deletion is timestamped. I'm not telling you what it means yet. I'm telling you it happened, and asking you to tell me what it means.'

Renée looked at the table. When she spoke, her voice was as level as ever, but something underneath it had gone careful.

'I approved the protocol,' she said. 'Eight months ago, with the information available then. The protocol was sound at the time. Conditions changed. The override, the schedule compression, the deferred recalibration—none of that existed when I signed. My approval was for a different operating envelope than the one that morning.'

'So why delete it?'

The room was silent.

'Because it has my name on it,' Renée said finally. 'Because the moment he died, I knew exactly how it would read. "Safety approved this." It doesn't matter that the conditions changed. It doesn't matter that I'd raised flags since. The headline writes itself: the safety officer signed off. And I have spent fifteen years being the one who's ignored, and I could not—' she stopped. 'I could not be the one who's blamed. Not for this. Not after being right so many times no one listened.'

'So you removed your own signature,' Ellery said.

'I removed a record that would be misread,' Renée said. 'A record that was true when I made it and false by the morning. That's not the same as hiding guilt.'

'It's exactly the same,' Ellery said, not unkindly. 'I understand why. I might even believe your version—that the approval was reasonable then. But you'll never get to make that argument now, because you deleted the document instead of defending it. An innocent record, destroyed, looks identical to a guilty one. You took a defensible signature and turned it into a cover-up with your own hand.'

Renée closed her eyes briefly.

'You widen the frame,' Ellery said. 'That's your method. You never argue the small point—you pull back until the bigger pattern is obvious. But this time you did the opposite. You narrowed it. You reached in and deleted one small thing, and made yourself look like everyone you've spent your career warning about.'

The room was very still.

'The report,' she said, 'will note that a prior safety approval was deleted after the incident, the timestamp of the deletion, and the account responsible. The original, recovered from the audit backup, will be attached.'

She stood.

'The recovered version helps you,' Ellery said. 'That's the irony. Read in full, in context, it's defensible—you approved a different envelope than the one that ran. If you'd left it alone, it might have cleared you. You destroyed the thing that would have saved you, because you couldn't stand to be seen holding it.'

She left both versions on the table—the deletion log, and the record it tried to erase.
