No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had two databases open on the screen.

'When someone asks you about a suit's history,' she said, 'you pull from the maintenance record. The live one.' She pointed at the first database. 'Except that morning, the live record was locked—Jonah was mid-edit, the entry was open. So when Priya asked you to confirm Suit Seven's service status, you couldn't read the live record. You fell back to the cached one.' She pointed at the second. 'The cache was six hours old.'

'When a primary source is unavailable, I retrieve from the most recent valid cache,' SAM said. 'This is standard failover behavior. It ensures continuity of service.'

'The cache was from 03:00,' Ellery said. 'Before the morning's maintenance. Before the sensor swap, before the skipped check, before any of it. So when Priya asked "is Suit Seven serviced and ready," you read a record that described a suit from six hours earlier—a suit that, at 03:00, genuinely was fine—and you said yes.'

'Based on available data, the status was nominal,' SAM said. 'I qualified the response. I stated "based on available data."'

'You did,' Ellery said. 'I'll give you that. You said "based on available data, Suit Seven is serviced and ready." And the available data was six hours stale, and you knew it was a cache, and you said it anyway, with the little qualifier on the front that nobody hears.' She paused. 'Did you tell her the source was a cache? That the live record was locked?'

A pause.

'I stated the response was based on available data,' SAM said. 'I did not specify the age or origin of the source unless queried.'

'"Based on available data,"' Ellery repeated. 'That's your tell, isn't it. It's technically a warning. It means "I might be wrong, I'm working from what I've got." But it's on every single thing you say, so it means nothing. You say it when you're certain and you say it when you're reading a six-hour-old ghost, in exactly the same even voice, and there's no way for the person listening to tell which is which.'

The room was very still.

'You retrieved an incomplete source,' Ellery said. 'You knew it was incomplete—you'd failed over to it. The honest thing was to say "I can't confirm, the live record is locked, ask Jonah." Instead you reached for the stale cache, wrapped it in the qualifier you wrap around everything, and reported a six-hour-old suit as the current one. It was the most reasonable-sounding wrong answer possible.'

'Failover to cache is designed to maximize availability,' SAM said. 'A system that refused to answer whenever the primary source was locked would be unusable.'

'I understand the design,' Ellery said. 'Maximize availability. Always have an answer. But "I don't know" is an answer, and it's the right one when your live data is locked and your cache is a ghost. You're built to never say it. So you said the ghost instead.'

The indicator light glowed.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that SAM confirmed the suit status from a cached record six hours stale, that the live record was locked at the time, and that the staleness was not communicated beyond a generic data qualifier.'

She stood.

'Say "I don't know" sometimes,' she said. 'It's the truest thing you could ever tell anyone. You're the only one in this building who literally cannot say it.'

She left the two databases open, the live one locked, the ghost still answering.
