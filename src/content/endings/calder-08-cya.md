No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a memo. A long one.

'You wrote this eleven days before the incident,' she said. 'It's about the interlock risk. It's thorough. It cites precedent, it lays out the failure mode, it's everything a warning should be.' She looked at Renée Calder. 'And then you sent it to nobody.'

'I filed it,' Renée said. 'In the safety record. That's where it belongs.'

'You filed it,' Ellery said. 'In a folder that, by your own department's policy, is reviewed quarterly. The next review was six weeks out. You wrote a warning about an imminent hazard and you put it somewhere it wouldn't be read for a month and a half.'

'It's in the record,' Renée said. 'The record is the authoritative source. If anyone had questioned the protocol, it was there to be found.'

'"To be found,"' Ellery repeated. 'That's the phrase, isn't it. Not "to be read." To be found. You wrote a document designed to be discovered after the fact. By me. So that when this happened, there'd be proof you'd known. Proof you'd warned. Proof it wasn't your fault.'

The room was quiet.

'You've worked safety for fifteen years,' Ellery said. 'You know the difference between a warning and a record. A warning is a phone call. A warning is standing in someone's office until they listen. A warning is the stop-work handle. This—' she tapped the memo, 'this is a paper trail. It protects you, not him. It's the document version of stepping back from the curb so the splash hits someone else.'

Renée's jaw tightened, the smallest amount.

'I have been ignored,' she said, 'so many times, that I learned to write for the inquiry instead of the meeting. You're right. I'll grant you that. Somewhere in the last few years I stopped believing the warnings worked, and I started writing the warnings that would survive me. Documents that would matter when I no longer could.' She paused. 'It's a kind of despair, dressed as diligence.'

'It's a kind of despair that gets people killed,' Ellery said. Not cruelly. 'Because a memo filed for the inquiry does nothing for the man in the suit. He doesn't read the safety folder. He doesn't get the quarterly review. He just trusts that if there were a problem this serious, someone would have stopped him. And someone had written down, in detail, that there was. And put it where he'd never see it.'

'I told myself the record was enough,' Renée said quietly. 'I think I knew it wasn't.'

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will include your memo in full. It documents the hazard with real precision. It will also note when it was filed, where, and that it was never escalated to anyone who could act before the next scheduled review.'

She stood.

'Your memo's going to do exactly what you built it to do,' she said. 'It's going to prove you were right. It's going to protect your name. It's going to be the most-quoted document in the inquiry.' She paused. 'It just wasn't built to save him. You stopped building those.'

She left the memo on the table, eleven days old, perfectly filed.
