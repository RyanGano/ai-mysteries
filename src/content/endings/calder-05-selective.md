No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a list of halts.

'You've stopped tests before,' she said. 'Many times. I pulled your record. Over three years, you invoked stop-work authority on forty-one occasions.' She looked at Renée Calder. 'I went through all forty-one. There's a pattern.'

'I halt when the conditions warrant it,' Renée said. 'The pattern is that I'm consistent.'

'You're not, though,' Ellery said. 'That's what's interesting. You halted Elias's runs for things that turned out minor. You halted Jonah twice for paperwork. But the runs tied to Priya's milestones—the high-visibility ones, the ones with money attached—you never halted. Not once. Even when the safety case was weaker than runs you'd stopped cold.'

Renée was quiet.

'The morning of the incident was a milestone run,' Ellery said. 'Priya's. Investor watching. Must-run flag. The exact category you've never touched in three years.' She paused. 'You halt the cheap tests and you wave through the expensive ones. Why?'

'Because halting the expensive ones doesn't work,' Renée said. Her voice was very controlled. 'You're describing it as cowardice. It's the opposite. Early on, I halted the high-stakes runs too. Do you know what happened? I was overruled within the hour, my authority was quietly clipped, and the test ran anyway, now with the safety officer on record as an obstacle to be managed. I learned that I have exactly as much halt authority as the cost of the run permits. On a cheap test, my stop sticks. On a milestone, it's theater.'

'So you stopped trying on the ones that mattered,' Ellery said.

'I stopped wasting authority I didn't actually have,' Renée said. 'There's a difference. I preserved my credibility for the fights I could win. That's not surrender. That's strategy.'

'It's strategy,' Ellery agreed. 'And the strategy meant that the runs most likely to kill someone—the rushed, expensive, must-run ones—were precisely the ones you'd decided were unwinnable. So you let them go. You triaged. You spent your authority where it would hold and withheld it where it wouldn't.' She paused. 'And the one that wouldn't hold was the one that mattered.'

The room was silent.

'You're going to tell me the halt wouldn't have stuck anyway,' Ellery said. 'And you might be right. Priya had the flag, the investor, the board. Maybe you'd have been overruled in an hour, like always.' She leaned forward. 'But you didn't try. You didn't make them overrule you. You looked at the run, recognized the category, and stayed in your chair, because you'd learned that on these ones your hands were tied.' She paused. 'Except your hands weren't tied that morning. You just believed they were. Belief was enough.'

'The report,' she said, 'will note your pattern of stop-work invocations. It will note the consistent exception for high-priority tests. It will note that no halt was invoked on the morning of the incident.'

She stood.

'You taught yourself which fights were real,' Ellery said. 'It was a reasonable thing to learn in a place like this. But you learned it so well that when the fight that was actually real arrived, you'd already filed it under the ones you don't take.'

She left the list of forty-one halts on the table, and the forty-second that wasn't there.
