No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery didn't bring a document. She brought a question.

'You believe this program will kill people,' she said. 'Not might. Will. At scale, once the suit ships. You've said it in writing. Thousands, you wrote once. If it goes to market with these margins, thousands.'

'I believe the evidence supports that,' Renée Calder said. 'I've believed it for two years. Nothing has happened to change my mind. If anything, the morning we're here to discuss confirmed it.'

'So let me ask you something,' Ellery said. 'And I want you to hear that I'm asking, not accusing. If you genuinely believed this program would kill thousands—and the only thing keeping it alive was funding—and the only thing that would stop the funding was a visible, undeniable safety failure—' she paused. 'What would a person who thought in those terms do with a morning like that one?'

The room went very still.

Renée didn't flinch. That was the unnerving thing. She considered it.

'You're asking whether I let it happen,' she said. 'To kill the program. One death to prevent thousands.'

'I'm asking,' Ellery said, 'whether the math ever occurred to you. Because it occurred to me, reading your files. You're a person who widens the frame. You think in populations. And a person who thinks in populations can do terrible arithmetic and call it conscience.'

'I had halt authority that morning,' Renée said slowly. 'I didn't use it. That's the fact you're circling. You want to know if the reason was fear, or fatigue, or—calculation.'

'Was it?'

Renée was quiet for a long moment.

'I don't know,' she said finally. And it was, somehow, worse than a confession. 'That's the honest answer, and you're the only person I'll ever give it to. I've gone over that morning a thousand times. I had the lever. I didn't pull it. And I cannot tell you, with certainty, whether I didn't pull it because I judged it wouldn't work—or because some part of me had already done the math and decided the program was more dangerous than the man.' She looked at her hands. 'I've spent my whole life able to tell exactly why I do things. This is the one time I can't. And I think the not-knowing is its own answer.'

'That's the most frightening thing anyone's said to me in this building,' Ellery said quietly.

'I know,' Renée said. 'It frightens me too. I am the person who's supposed to be incorruptible. And the corruption, if it's there, didn't come from money or fear. It came from caring too much about the wrong number of people.'

The room held its silence.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that you held unused halt authority on the morning of the incident, and that you had documented a belief that the program posed a mass-casualty risk. It will not speculate on motive. There's nothing to speculate with. Only you know, and you've just told me you don't.'

She stood.

'Pull the handle on the next one,' she said at the door. 'Whatever the math says. Pull it. Don't let yourself find out which kind of person you are by waiting for the body to tell you.'

She left.

Renée sat very still, in the frame she had widened until a single man disappeared inside it.
