No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery looked up at the ceiling panel, where the indicator light glowed its pale blue.

'SAM,' she said. 'I asked you a question on the first day, and I've thought about your answer ever since. I asked you to estimate the probability that the people in this room conspired to kill Michael Holloway. Do you remember your answer?'

'I do,' SAM said. The voice was even. 'I estimated the probability of deliberate collective coordination as low.'

'Low,' Ellery repeated. 'I believed you. You were right. There was no conspiracy. No plan. No meeting.' She looked around the table, at the five of them. 'And he's still dead, and you all still did it. So I came back to you with a different question. SAM—what's the probability that five people, each acting alone, each making one reasonable decision, would produce this exact outcome?'

A pause. Shorter than a human pause, longer than a processing delay.

'Higher,' SAM said. 'Substantially higher.'

'Say the number.'

'Given the operating conditions on the morning in question—the schedule pressure, the deferred maintenance, the suppressed alerts, the narrowed margins, the unenforced halt—the probability of an adverse outcome from the combination of independent decisions was approximately one in three.'

The room went very still.

'One in three,' Ellery said. 'Not a conspiracy. A coin weighted two-to-one against a man's life, flipped by five people who each thought they were holding only one edge of it.' She paused. 'You knew that, SAM. You could have run that number any morning. Did anyone ask you?'

'No one asked me to evaluate the combination,' SAM said. 'Each input was queried individually. Each was within parameters. I am not asked to synthesize risks across independent decision-makers. I answer the questions I am asked.'

'And no one asked the only question that mattered,' Ellery said. She looked at the five of them. 'That's not SAM's failure. SAM told the truth every time it was asked. It's yours. Five people who each asked "is my part okay" and never once asked "are we okay." The machine would have told you. It just told you it was one in three, right now, when I asked. It would have told any of you, any morning. You only had to ask the question none of you wanted the answer to.'

No one spoke.

'I keep thinking about how clean it is,' Ellery said. 'No one has to be evil. No one has to plan. You just need five reasonable people, a system that answers narrow questions honestly, and a culture where nobody asks the wide one. That's not a crime. That's an operating model. You'd built a machine for producing this, and it produced it, exactly on schedule, one time in three.'

'The report,' she said, 'will include SAM's probability estimate. It will note that the combined risk was both calculable and uncalculated. It will note that the information existed and the question did not.'

She stood.

'Ask it next time,' she said, to all of them and none of them. 'The wide question. I know you won't. But ask it.'

The indicator light glowed its pale, even blue. It had been glowing the whole time.
