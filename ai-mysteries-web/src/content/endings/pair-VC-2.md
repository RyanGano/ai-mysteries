No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a simulation and a memo.

'Eleven days before the incident,' she said, 'Elias, you ran a failure model. Forty-one times. Three of the runs predicted a fatality. You wrote it up as "low-probability, non-zero, merits further investigation," and you filed it in drafts.' She turned. 'Renée, eleven days before the incident, you wrote a stop-work memo about the same hazard. And you filed it where it wouldn't be read for six weeks.'

The two of them were silent.

'You both knew,' Ellery said. 'Independently. From completely different directions. Elias, you knew from the math. Renée, you knew from the pattern. The most analytical person in the building and the most experienced safety mind in the building both saw this coming, eleven days out, and both of you wrote it down, and both of you put it somewhere it would be found after instead of acted on before.'

'I had not validated the model,' Varga said. 'Preliminary findings are not escalated.'

'And my memo required a countersignature I knew I wouldn't get,' Renée said.

'You each had a reason,' Ellery said. 'You always do—you're the careful ones, your reasons are good. But look at what happened. The two people who saw it coming both chose to document instead of intervene. If either of you had walked your warning into the right room and refused to leave—if Elias had escalated the unvalidated model, if Renée had pulled the handle on an uncountersigned memo—someone would have had to look. Two warnings, loud, eleven days early. The test doesn't run.'

'Escalating every preliminary result would destroy the credibility of escalation,' Varga said.

'Pulling the handle on every uncountersigned concern would have ended my authority years ago,' Renée said.

'I know,' Ellery said. 'And here's the terrible thing. If you'd known about each other, it changes everything. Elias, if you'd known Renée had independently reached the same conclusion from the safety data—that's validation, that's the thing you said you were waiting for. Renée, if you'd known Elias's model put a number on it—that's the countersignature you needed, in spirit. Two warnings that each lacked exactly what the other one had.'

The room was very still.

'You're the two who knew,' Ellery said. 'Not the careless ones. The two careful, brilliant, cautious people who each saw the future clearly, wrote it down precisely, and filed it quietly, eleven days before it came true. Alone, each warning was incomplete. Together, they were proof. And you never put them together, because you each thought you were the only one who saw.'

'The report,' she said, 'will include both the simulation and the memo. It will note that two independent, predictive warnings existed eleven days prior, and that neither was escalated, and that neither author knew of the other.'

She stood.

'Talk to each other,' she said. 'That's all. The two smartest, most careful people in the building each had half of the warning that would have saved him. You just never compared notes.'

She left the simulation and the memo together, eleven days too late.
