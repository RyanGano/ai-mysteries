No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a folder she hadn't been given. She'd found it.

'You've been keeping a file,' she said. 'On the team. Months of it. Dated entries. Priya's schedule overrides. Elias's calibration changes. Jonah's checklists. Marcus's access logs. Every cut corner, every near-miss, every time someone chose the schedule over the margin.' She looked at Renée Calder. 'It's meticulous. It's the best account of what was going wrong in this building that exists anywhere.'

'I document,' Renée said. 'That's the role. Patterns only become visible if someone writes them down as they happen. A safety officer who doesn't keep records isn't doing the job.'

'This isn't a safety log,' Ellery said. 'A safety log goes into the system, where people can act on it. This is private. On your own drive. You weren't recording these to fix them. You were recording them to prove something. Later. To someone.'

Renée didn't answer.

'You'd given up,' Ellery said. 'That's what the file tells me. You'd raised the alarm so many times and been ignored so many times that you stopped expecting anyone to listen. So you changed strategy. You stopped trying to prevent the disaster and started building the case for after it. You weren't documenting to stop it. You were documenting because you knew it was coming and you wanted to be the one holding the proof.'

'That's not—' Renée began, and then she stopped, because she was an honest person, and the honest thing was harder.

'You knew it was coming,' Ellery said. 'You say it yourself, in the entries. "This is how it starts." "I've seen this before." You weren't speculating. You were watching a fuse burn and writing down the length of it.' She paused. 'And every day you wrote a new entry instead of pulling the handle.'

The room was silent.

'You have halt authority,' Ellery said. 'On paper. You could have stopped the program. Any day in this file, you could have invoked it, taken the fight to the board, forced the shutdown, been ignored loudly instead of quietly. It might have cost you your job. It might have failed. But you had the lever, and instead of pulling it, you wrote it down.'

'They would have overruled me,' Renée said quietly. 'You don't understand what it's like to be right in a room where being right doesn't matter. I had pulled the handle before. It does nothing. It just marks you as the obstacle. So yes. I documented. So that when it happened—and it was going to happen—someone would finally have to look.'

'And someone finally has to look,' Ellery said. 'That's true. Your file is going to change this place. People will be held to account because of it. You'll be proven right, completely, devastatingly right.' She paused. 'And a man had to die for your file to matter. You knew that trade when you opened the folder.'

Renée's composure didn't break. But it cost her something not to.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will reference your private documentation of the safety deterioration. It will note that the patterns you recorded predicted the incident. It will note that you held halt authority throughout the period documented.'

She stood.

'You'll be the hero of the inquiry,' she said. 'The one who saw it coming. And only you and I will know that seeing it coming and choosing to watch are not the same as trying to stop it.'

She left the folder on the table. Every entry dated. Every prediction correct.
