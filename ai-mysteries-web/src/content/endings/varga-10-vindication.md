No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery did not bring a document this time.

'I want to talk about being right,' she said. 'You've spent two years telling everyone this program was reckless. The timeline was irresponsible. The science wasn't ready. You said it in meetings, in memos, in the margins of other people's reports.' She looked at Elias Varga. 'You were right.'

'I'm aware,' he said.

'You said there would be an incident.'

'I said the probability of an incident was being systematically understated. Which is a different and more precise claim. But—yes. In substance.'

'So when the morning came,' Ellery said, 'and you saw the schedule pressure, and the deferred recalibration, and Renée's memo sitting unanswered, and the anomalous reading—you knew. Better than anyone in this building, you knew what was assembling.'

Varga said nothing.

'And you let it run,' she said.

The room went still.

'That's a serious accusation,' Varga said. 'I want you to be precise about it, since you're being imprecise. I had no authority to halt the test. That was Renée's. I had no control over the schedule. That was Priya's. I am a scientist. I raise concerns. I had raised every concern I had, repeatedly, in writing, and been ignored, repeatedly. What exactly was I to do that morning that I had not already done a hundred times?'

'You could have stood in the doorway,' Ellery said.

He looked at her.

'Not metaphorically,' she said. 'Literally. You could have walked into the Charge Cage and stood there and refused to move until someone listened. You've never lacked the conviction. You lacked—' she paused. 'You wanted to be right more than you wanted to be wrong about the danger. And you can only be proven right if it happens.'

'That is monstrous,' Varga said quietly. 'That is a monstrous thing to say to me.'

'Yes,' Ellery said. 'It is. I've thought about whether to say it. I decided you'd rather hear it than have it left unsaid. You're a man who'd rather have the accurate version.' She paused. 'Tell me I'm wrong.'

He opened his mouth. Then he closed it. He looked at the table, and at his hands, and at the empty place at the end of it where Michael Holloway used to sit.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that you raised every concern through every proper channel. It will commend you for it. It will be entirely true.'

She stood.

'It won't say the rest,' she said. 'Neither of us will. But you'll know I know. And you'll know that being right was always going to cost exactly this much. I think you knew that going in.'

She left him there, vindicated, in the quiet.
