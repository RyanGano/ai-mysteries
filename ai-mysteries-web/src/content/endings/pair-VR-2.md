No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a gap in the footage and a gap in the timestamps.

'There's no video of the incident,' she said. 'A manual storage cycle—Marcus, that's yours. And the telemetry that would let me reconstruct it without video is unusable, because the clocks were four hundred milliseconds out of sync—Elias, that's yours.' She looked between them. 'Between the two of you, there's no way to see what happened in that bay. No picture, and no numbers I can trust.'

'The storage cycle was routine,' Rourke said.

'The sync adjustment was an optimization,' Varga said.

'I'm sure they both were,' Ellery said. 'Here's what interests me. When I started this investigation, I had two ways to reconstruct that morning. The cameras and the data. Standard. You always have at least one. I have neither.' She paused. 'And the two things that took them away were done by two different people who don't work together, for two different reasons, that happen to combine into the one thing an investigator can't work around: a morning with no record.'

'That's coincidence,' Varga said. 'You're constructing a conspiracy out of two ordinary maintenance actions that happen to inconvenience you.'

'Maybe,' Ellery said. 'I've thought hard about coincidence. A camera gap, alone—happens. A clock skew, alone—happens. But the camera gap erases the picture, and the clock skew erases the data, and together they erase the morning completely, and the two of them landed on the same morning, the only morning that mattered.' She looked at Rourke. 'You're a man who cleans up records.' She looked at Varga. 'You're a man who can't stand imprecise data, except this once.'

The room was quiet.

'I'm not saying you did it together,' Ellery said. 'I genuinely don't think you did. I think you each, separately, made the one change that blinds your own domain—Marcus the cameras, Elias the clocks. And the universe, or the building, or just bad luck, arranged for both blindnesses to fall on the same hour.' She paused. 'But I'll be honest: when two people who don't talk both happen to erase their own kind of evidence on the day a man dies, I stop believing in luck and start believing in habit. Yours, Marcus, is to leave no record. Yours, Elias, is to trust the model over the mess. Together they made a morning no one can ever see.'

'The report,' she said, 'will note the unavailability of both video and reliable telemetry for the incident window, the separate causes of each, and the two individuals responsible.'

She stood.

'I can't show you what happened in that bay,' she said. 'Neither of you left me anything to show. And I think that, more than the death, is the thing that ties you two together—you both, in your different ways, made sure there'd be nothing to see.'

She left the two gaps where they were.
