No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a maintenance flag and a stop-work memo.

'Marcus,' she said, 'at 07:14 you filed a maintenance flag that suppressed the proximity-confirmation layer.' She turned. 'Renée, at 08:17 you wrote a stop-work memo about the interlock and filed it where it wouldn't be read in time.' She set them down. 'One of you took away the system's eyes. One of you held back the warning that the system was blind.'

Neither spoke.

'Here's what gets me,' Ellery said. 'Renée, your memo names the interlock vulnerability almost exactly. You understood that the presence detection could be suppressed and that suppression was dangerous. You wrote it down at 08:17.' She turned to Rourke. 'And Marcus, you'd suppressed the presence layer at 07:14. An hour before she wrote the warning, you'd already done the thing she was warning about.'

The room went quiet.

'Renée,' Ellery said, 'if your memo had gone to the right person at 08:17 instead of into a folder—if you'd walked it to Marcus, or to operations—someone would have checked whether the presence layer was, in fact, suppressed. It was. They'd have found Marcus's flag. They'd have restored it. The test waits, the man lives.' She paused. 'Your warning and his action were about the exact same hole. You just never connected them, because his action was buried in a maintenance log and your warning was buried in a safety folder, and the two of you don't read each other's filing systems.'

'My flag was routine,' Rourke said.

'My memo was filed correctly,' Renée said.

'Both true,' Ellery said. 'And between you, you created a blind room and then declined to ring the bell about it. Marcus blinded the system. Renée knew the system could be blinded and wrote it down where it would help no one in time. His was the hole. Yours was the unsounded alarm about the hole. The same hole. An hour apart.'

'I had no way to know his specific flag was active,' Renée said.

'No,' Ellery agreed. 'And he had no way to know your memo existed. That's the whole tragedy of this building in one exchange. The man who made the hazard and the woman who diagnosed it were sixty minutes and one hallway apart, and neither knew the other was there. You were both right about the danger. Neither of you put your rightness where the other could use it.'

'The report,' she said, 'will note the maintenance flag suppressing the presence layer, the stop-work memo identifying that exact vulnerability, the one-hour gap between them, and that the two were never connected.'

She stood.

'You described his mistake,' she told Renée, 'an hour after he made it, and filed the description in a drawer.' She turned to Rourke. 'And you made the mistake she'd have caught, if she'd ever shown anyone. You were so close to saving him. You just weren't looking at each other.'

She left the flag and the memo on the table, an hour apart.
