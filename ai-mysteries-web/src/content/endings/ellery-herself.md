No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

There was no detective at the head of the table.

For eleven weeks the chair had been hers. Mara Ellery had sat in it and laid documents down two at a time and asked the question that turned the room—the one that made each of them, in turn, look at the table as though there were something written on it. They had come to think of the chair as belonging to the investigation itself. It did not occur to any of them, until the chair was empty, that an investigation is only ever a person.

Priya Shah had the master file open in front of her. Not the version that had been shared with the team. The other one—the working file, recovered from the case-management server after Ellery's credentials went dark on a Friday and did not come back on the Monday.

'There are forty of these,' Priya said. 'More. I stopped counting.'

She turned the screen so the table could see. Reconstruction after reconstruction. A timeline that put it on Varga's variance. A timeline that put it on Rourke's rounds. One that named Calder's unfiled memo. One that named no one—that named the model, SAM, the thing that had no hands. Each document complete. Each internally perfect. Each one naming a different culprit, and each one mutually, surgically exclusive of all the others.

'She wrote every version,' Marcus Rourke said. It was not a question.

'She wrote a version for every one of us,' Priya said. 'And every pair of us. And every three.' She scrolled. 'So that whoever opened the file would find a suspect. Whoever looked would find an answer. The answer would just never be the same one twice.'

Jonah Pike said the thing none of them wanted to be first to say. 'Then who introduced the fault.'

Renée Calder had the maintenance log. She had been holding it, folded, for most of the meeting. She set it down now and smoothed it flat.

'The calibration offset was loaded the night before the test,' she said. 'From a terminal on the operations floor. Badge access—a contractor's card, dormant for months, re-activated that afternoon against a temporary credential.' She paused. 'The credential was issued for a pre-test safety review. An outside sign-off, the kind we bring in before we go live.' She smoothed the page again, though it was already flat. 'In the copy of the file that was shared with us, the reviewer's name is redacted. In the copy she kept for herself, it isn't.'

The room held very still.

It was Elias Varga, finally, who reached the part that mattered, and he reached it quietly, the way a person arrives at something they have known for a while without letting themselves know it.

'We've met her before,' he said. 'Not here. Earlier than here.'

Priya did not look up from the file. 'There was a near-miss. Four years ago, before any of us. A smaller test, a smaller failure. It was closed as within tolerance.' She was reading now, flatly, the way Ellery herself used to read. 'The technician who flagged it was overruled. He left the industry. His name is in the closure report, two lines down, as the dissenting signature.' She turned the screen back toward herself. 'The investigator who signed off on closing it—the one who wrote that the residual risk was acceptable—was a junior safety auditor named Mara Ellery.'

No one said anything.

'She spent four years deciding she'd been wrong,' Renée said. 'And then she made a failure that couldn't be absorbed. And then she made sure the system would have somebody to absorb instead.'

'Any of us,' Marcus said.

'All of us,' Priya said. 'Interchangeably. That was the point. As long as there was always an answer, no one would go looking for the question.'

Outside the building the light was doing the ordinary thing it did in the late afternoon. Somewhere, by now—weeks gone, no forwarding address, a phone that rang once into nothing and then not at all—a woman who had once signed her name under the words _acceptable loss_ was sitting where the work could not reach her, watching a tide come in and go out and come in again, each pass taking a little of the shore and giving a little back, the whole arrangement balancing to zero, well within tolerance.

The system had made a decision.

She had simply been the one who decided to stop trusting it—and then, with great care, the one who made certain no one else would ever get the chance to.
