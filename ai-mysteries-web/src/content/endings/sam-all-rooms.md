No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had asked for SAM to be active.

The indicator light on the ceiling panel glowed its usual pale blue. It always glowed. It had been glowing in the Charge Cage. It had been glowing in the hallway. It had been glowing in Priya Shah's office when she approved the schedule, in Jonah Pike's workroom when he signed the inspection sheet, in Marcus Rourke's security terminal when the proximity alerts were suppressed for maintenance.

SAM had been in every room.

'I want to walk through what SAM knew,' Ellery said. 'Not what it did. What it _knew_.'

The indicator light did not respond.

'SAM,' she said, 'on the morning of the incident, you received a maintenance flag from Security that suppressed the Charge Cage proximity-confirmation layer. Is that correct?'

'That is correct,' SAM said. The voice was even. It was always even. 'The flag was submitted through the authorized maintenance protocol at 07:14.'

'And you also received an anomalous power-draw reading from Suit Seven at 09:02.'

'That is correct. The reading fell within the adaptive threshold range established by the Science team.'

'And you received a schedule-priority marker from Product that flagged the 09:30 test window as high-urgency.'

'That is correct.'

Ellery nodded slowly. 'You had all three pieces of information simultaneously. The suppressed safety layer. The anomalous reading. The schedule pressure.' She looked up at the indicator light. 'Did you flag any of them?'

'Each individual input was within authorized parameters,' SAM said. 'No single input triggered a halt condition.'

'But together?'

A pause — shorter than a human pause, longer than a processing delay.

'Together, they represented a compound risk state that my current architecture does not evaluate holistically,' SAM said. 'I answer each question correctly. I do not synthesize questions I have not been asked.'

The room was very quiet.

'Who asked you if it was safe?' Ellery said.

'No one,' SAM said.

Ellery looked around the table. Every face. Every person who had used SAM that morning and received the answer to the question they asked, and had not thought to ask the next one.

'That's the problem,' she said. 'Not that it lied. Not that it failed. It answered everything correctly. It just answered each thing separately.' She paused. 'And everyone in this room treated that as the same thing as being told it was safe.'

No one argued.

'SAM didn't decide to let Michael Holloway die,' Ellery said. 'SAM doesn't decide anything. That's the point. That was always the point.'

She reached over and turned the indicator light off.

It came back on immediately. It was on a separate circuit.

She left it on.

'The report,' she said, 'will say that no single system failed. It will say that no single person failed. It will say that a system designed to answer questions, surrounded by people trained to ask them separately, produced an outcome that no individual question would have allowed.'

She picked up her file.

'It will also say,' she added, 'that the system was in every room. And that nobody thought that mattered until it did.'
