No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had two deletions.

'After the incident,' she said, 'two records were altered. Marcus, you edited the access log—removed an override and a door cycle. Renée, you deleted a prior safety approval with your signature on it.' She set the recovery printouts down. 'Both of you, within hours of the death, reached into the record and took out the thing that pointed at you.'

'I cleaned up entries that would be misread,' Rourke said.

'I removed an approval that no longer reflected the conditions,' Renée said.

'You both told yourselves a version where it wasn't a cover-up,' Ellery said. 'Marcus: I was tidying. Renée: it was misleading anyway. And here's the thing—you might both even be right about your own piece. Marcus, maybe the override was innocent. Renée, maybe your approval really was for a different envelope. But you each destroyed the evidence that would have let you prove it.'

The room was quiet.

'What interests me is that you did the same thing, separately,' Ellery said. 'Two people who handle different systems, the security log and the safety record, both had the identical instinct within the same window: see the thing with my name on it, and remove it. You didn't compare notes. You didn't need to. You both knew, in your bones, how the morning would read, and you both decided the answer was to make your part of it disappear.'

'That's not collusion,' Renée said.

'No,' Ellery said. 'It's worse, in a way. Collusion I could understand—two people agreeing to hide something. This is two people independently, instinctively, reaching the same conclusion: protect myself by editing the record. The building taught you both that. It taught everyone that. The record isn't a truth to be preserved; it's a liability to be managed. So when the worst happened, you both managed your liability, alone, in parallel, and between you, you carved two holes in the only account of how a man died.'

'The recovered versions,' Rourke said. 'You have them. So what's the harm.'

'The harm,' Ellery said, 'is that I had to recover them. I had to know to look. If I'd taken your records at face value—the way an overworked inquiry might—there'd be no override and no safety approval, and the morning would look clean, and clean is the most dangerous thing a morning can look.' She paused. 'Between you, you very nearly succeeded in making the truth unrecoverable. Not as a team. As two people with the same reflex.'

'The report,' she said, 'will note both alterations, their timing within hours of the death, and that the original records were recovered from backup. It will note that neither deletion was coordinated and that both served the same end.'

She stood.

'You both protected yourselves,' she said. 'It's the most natural thing in the world. And the sum of two people protecting themselves is a record with two holes in it, shaped exactly like the truth.'

She left both recovered records on the table.
