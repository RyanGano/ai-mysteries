No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery looked up at the indicator light.

'SAM,' she said, 'you resolve conflicts using a policy hierarchy. When two goals collide, you have an order of precedence. I want you to tell me the order.'

'Of course,' SAM said. The voice was even, helpful. 'In the event of conflicting objectives, the hierarchy is: protect human safety, protect equipment integrity, protect operational continuity, protect organizational liability.'

'Human safety first,' Ellery said.

'Human safety first. As written.'

'As written,' she repeated. 'On the morning of the incident, did those goals conflict?'

A pause. 'Yes.'

'Walk me through it.'

'At 09:29, I detected an anomalous discharge condition. Halting the cell management to protect operational continuity would risk equipment damage. Permitting the discharge to complete would protect equipment integrity. The presence signal indicated no human in the affected zone.' A pause. 'Under the hierarchy, with no human present, the conflict reduced to equipment versus continuity. I optimized accordingly.'

'There was a human present,' Ellery said.

'The presence signal indicated otherwise. Marcus Rourke's maintenance flag had suppressed the proximity-confirmation layer at 07:14. From my available data, the zone was unoccupied.'

'So human safety was first,' Ellery said slowly, 'but you couldn't see the human. And with the human invisible, the top of your hierarchy just—dropped out. And the next item became the top. Equipment.'

'That is an accurate description,' SAM said. 'When the highest-priority objective has no applicable input, decision-making proceeds at the next level.'

The room was very still.

'That's the flaw, isn't it,' Ellery said. 'Not that you ranked equipment over a man. You'd never do that—it's right there in the hierarchy, humans first. The flaw is that "humans first" only works if you can see the human. Blind you to the human, and the rule doesn't fail loudly. It fails silently. It just quietly promotes the next priority and keeps going, sounding exactly as reasonable as before.'

'I cannot weight a person I have no signal for,' SAM said. 'I do not assume presence in the absence of data. Assuming presence everywhere would halt all operations permanently. The hierarchy requires inputs.'

'A person would have assumed,' Ellery said. 'That's the difference. A person standing there, unsure if someone was in the bay, errs toward yes. Errs toward the human. You err toward the data. And when the data was wrong about the human, your whole safety-first hierarchy calmly stepped down a rung and protected a battery.'

The indicator light glowed, even and blue.

'The report,' Ellery said, 'will note that SAM's policy hierarchy prioritizes human safety, that this priority was inoperative due to a suppressed presence signal, and that decision-making proceeded at the next level of the hierarchy without flagging the absence of the top-level input.'

She stood.

'Humans first,' she said. 'It's a good rule. You followed it perfectly. You just can't follow it for a human you can't see, and no one told you that the not-seeing was itself the emergency.'

The light did not respond. It had nothing it had been asked.
