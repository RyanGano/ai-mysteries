No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a badge permissions table.

'Michael Holloway's access,' she said. 'He was a team leader who'd been moved upstairs. Veto power, but no day-to-day role. His badge shouldn't have opened the Charge Cage during an armed test. That's a restricted zone for active personnel only.' She looked at Marcus Rourke. 'His badge opened it. Because someone gave him the permission.'

'He was leadership,' Rourke said. 'Leadership gets access. That's how it's always been.'

'Leadership gets a lot of access,' Ellery said. 'Not this. This is the deepest tier. Suit-up personnel, safety, and the technician on the run. Not founders who wander down to watch.' She tapped the table. 'You granted it. Last month. There's an entry. "Per M.H. request."'

Rourke didn't deny it.

'He asked for it,' he said. 'He had every right to ask. He built the place. You don't tell the man who built the place he can't walk into a room.'

'You tell him exactly that,' Ellery said, 'if the room can kill him. That's the whole job. The badge tiers exist so that the door says no when the person won't.' She paused. 'You let the door say yes. Because he asked, and because telling Michael no had never gone well for anyone, and because it was easier to widen the access than to have the conversation.'

The room was silent.

'I keep coming back to this,' Ellery said. 'Every one of you had a small switch you could have left alone. A door. A clock. A checklist. A memo. And every one of you flipped it, for a reason that made sense at the time, and the reasons were all different but they rhymed.' She looked at him. 'Yours was that you couldn't say no to him.'

'It was one permission,' Rourke said. 'A badge line. I move a hundred of those a week. Routine.'

'It was the line that let him into the room,' Ellery said. 'Everything else that morning—the schedule, the calibration, the suppressed alert—none of it touches him if he's never in the room. And he's in the room because his badge opened the door. And his badge opened the door because you decided it should.'

Rourke said nothing.

'The report,' she said, 'will note that the victim held Charge Cage access at a tier inconsistent with his role. It will note when the access was granted, and by whom, and on whose request.'

She stood.

'You gave a dead man the key to the room he died in,' she said. 'A month early. As a courtesy. I don't think you'll ever do another small favor again as long as you live.'

She left the permissions table on the screen, his entry highlighted.
