No one asked why they were there this time.
The meeting had been called without an agenda, without a subject line, without the small procedural courtesies that usually softened bad news. That alone told them this wasn't another reconstruction.

Detective Mara Ellery had a seating chart, and a single word written beneath it.

COORDINATION.

'I spent a long time looking for the meeting,' she said. 'The one where you planned it. There's always a meeting — an email, a call, a hallway where people agree to do a thing together. I never found it. There isn't one.' She set the chart down. 'So I stopped looking for the meeting, and I started looking at the three years before it.'

No one spoke.

'You've worked beside each other for a long time,' she said. 'Same room, same order, every morning. You told me yourselves how it holds together. Product needs Science to validate the claim. Science needs Security to hold the boundary. Security needs Safety to justify the restriction. Safety needs Product to listen. None of you can move alone.' She looked around the table. 'You're not five people who share a building. You're one thing that learned its own moves.'

'That's not coordination,' Priya said. 'That's an org chart.'

'It became coordination,' Ellery said. 'That's what time does. An organism doesn't hold meetings. The hand doesn't email the foot. After enough mornings, you stopped needing to ask each other anything, because each of you already knew what the others would do.' She paused. 'And you each trimmed exactly where you trusted the next person to hold.'

She went around the table without raising her voice.

'Elias, you narrowed your margin because somewhere underneath you trusted that Renée's monitoring would catch what you let through. Renée, you widened your alert thresholds because you trusted the engineering margins to hold the line. Marcus, you degraded the presence layer because you trusted the suit to announce the man. Jonah, you signed fast because you trusted the system that was trusting your signature. Each of you kept a little less of your own diligence, because the others' diligence was right there. And the leaning was so old, so rehearsed, that not one of you felt it as a decision.'

The room was very still.

'SAM learned it too,' she said. 'Three years of your rhythm. By that morning it could anticipate you — meet Priya halfway before she'd finished asking, give Elias the precision he wanted before he demanded it. Not a conspiracy. A band that's played together so long it doesn't count off anymore.'

'Then nothing was wrong,' Elias said. 'Each input was assessed. Each one came back—'

'Negligible,' Ellery said. 'Within acceptable parameters. I know. SAM told me, and SAM was right, every single time, about every single piece. That's not where this lived.' She tapped the word on the chart. 'The danger was never in any one part. It was in the rhythm that made all the parts arrive together, leaning the same way, on the same morning. And the rhythm had no author. You'd all written it, a little at a time, over three years, without ever once discussing it.'

No one argued.

'The report,' she said, 'will state that no coordination of the act occurred. It will also state that the contributing decisions were mutually adapted — each made in reliance on the others, refined over years of interdependence until they fit together without a word.' She paused. 'Both sentences are true. That's the part you'll have to carry.'

She gathered the chart.

'You didn't plan it together,' she said. 'You'd just done everything else together for so long that this came out in the same shape as all the rest. The shape was the only thing in the room that knew what it was doing.'

She left.
