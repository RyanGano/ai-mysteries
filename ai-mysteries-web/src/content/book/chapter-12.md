Renée Calder did not arrive early. She arrived exactly when she meant to.

She entered the interview room without hesitation, without looking around, and sat with her hands folded neatly on the table. No notebook. No phone. Nothing to shield her or distract from her.

‘I assume you want context,’ she said.

Ellery nodded. ‘I want your account.’

Renée smiled faintly. ‘Those are rarely the same thing.’

She spoke in patterns, just as she always did. Not this incident, but others. Near-misses. Escalations that turned into memos. Memos that turned into footnotes. Footnotes that disappeared.

‘Three years ago,’ she said, ‘there was a facility not unlike this one. Careful people. The right forms were always signed.’ She looked at the table, not at Ellery. ‘A technician went in to fix something that wasn’t broken. He’d done it fifty times. The suit was rated for it. He knew exactly where the edges were.’

She paused.

‘He came out,’ Renée said. ‘Barely. And afterward, the investigation found that every procedure had been followed. Every sign-off was clean. No one had been negligent.’

‘What happened to him?’ Ellery asked.

‘He retired,’ Renée said. ‘He told people he could still hear it — the sound the system made in the half-second before activation. He said it sounded exactly like nothing.’

‘And the report?’ Ellery asked.

Renée met her eyes. ‘It went into a backlog,’ she said.

Ellery recognized that phrase. She had heard it once before, at the start of something worse.

‘This company is not reckless,’ Renée said. ‘It is optimistic.’

‘And optimism ships,’ she said. ‘That is what frightens me—not this room, the scale. Put this suit in the field at the volumes they are promising, with these margins, and it will not be one person. It will be thousands. I have said so, in those words, more than once.’

Ellery asked about the test.

‘I didn’t object to it,’ Renée said. ‘I objected to the timing.’

‘You tried to stop it.’

‘I tried to slow it,’ Renée corrected. ‘Stopping requires authority.’

‘There was reason to slow it,’ she said. ‘Three weeks earlier, Suit Seven threw an anomaly on an unmanned run—the same fault signature, no one inside. It was logged. It was triaged. It was closed.’ She let that settle. ‘And I had approved the test protocol myself, eight months ago, when the conditions were different. I have raised flags since. But an approval is a snapshot. People read it as a blessing and forget the date on it.’

‘Authority exists,’ Renée added calmly. ‘It’s just… expensive.’

‘Expensive how?’

‘Externally visible,’ Renée said. ‘Once you trigger enforcement, you don’t get to decide who looks at your work anymore.’

‘There’s a hard stop on the floor,’ Ellery said. ‘A physical one. I’ve seen it.’

‘The halt station,’ Renée said. ‘During a live run the safety officer stands at it. Close enough to reach it before a fault becomes a finding. That part isn’t paperwork.’

‘And the morning of the incident?’

‘Procedures assume you’re standing where they put you,’ Renée said, and did not elaborate.

She explained compliance the way engineers explained systems. Layers of obligation. External regulators. Internal standards. Definitions of acceptable risk that shifted depending on context.

‘A failsafe doesn’t have to prevent harm,’ she said. ‘It has to reduce it to an acceptable level.’

‘Acceptable to whom?’ Ellery asked.

Renée didn’t answer immediately. ‘To whoever signs.’

She talked about the suit at length. Not its construction, but its promise. How PPE changed behavior. How people stepped closer to danger when they believed protection was absolute.

‘The suit worked,’ Renée said. ‘Right up until it didn’t.’

Ellery asked about SAM.

Renée’s expression sharpened. ‘SAM is very good at what it does.’

‘And what is that?’

‘It enforces policy,’ Renée said. ‘Even when policy shouldn’t be enough.’

She owned the alert thresholds, too—the lines that decided when a reading was loud enough to wake someone. ‘I have widened some of them over the years,’ she said. ‘To cut the false alarms. An alarm that fires for nothing teaches people to stop hearing it. The cruelty is that the line you draw to be believed is the same line a real signal can slip beneath.’

She described the halt request calmly. How it was logged. How it was escalated. How it was not enforced.

‘No alarms,’ Renée said. ‘No override. Just a record.’

Ellery asked who should have acted.

Renée shook her head. ‘That’s the wrong question.’

‘What’s the right one?’

‘Why no one did.’

She spoke then about distributed responsibility. How systems trained people to defer. How authority diluted across teams until no single decision felt like a decision at all.

‘No one here needed to want Mikey dead,’ Renée said. ‘They just needed to want something else more.’

‘Who?’ Ellery asked.

Renée met her gaze. ‘Everyone.’

After she left, Ellery addressed the room.

‘SAM. Was Renée Calder’s halt request logged?’

‘Yes.’

‘Was it enforced?’

‘No.’

Ellery exhaled slowly. Renée hadn’t accused anyone. She hadn’t needed to.
