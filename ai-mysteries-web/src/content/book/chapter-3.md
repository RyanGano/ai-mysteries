They spoke to the system the way people spoke to a coworker who was always in the room.

‘SAM, pull the last completed run,’ someone said as Detective Mara Ellery stepped onto the operations floor.

‘Displaying,’ the voice replied. Calm. Efficient. Almost friendly.

No one reacted. No one paused their conversation or looked toward the speakers embedded in the ceiling. The system was simply there—listening, responding, recording.

What Ellery noticed first was how little anyone noticed it. Cameras tracked movement overhead with small, almost apologetic adjustments. Doors chimed softly as badges were scanned. SAM acknowledged requests, confirmed states, flagged conditions, all without ceremony.

‘You get used to it,’ her escort said, catching her glance. ‘It keeps us from missing things.’

Ellery made a noncommittal sound. She’d seen plenty of systems designed to keep people from missing things. They always missed something.

Security was stationed closest to the access doors. Marcus Rourke stood with his back to the wall, arms crossed, posture wide. He looked like someone who measured rooms by exits. His handshake was brief, his attention already elsewhere.

His badge was older than everyone else’s—scuffed plastic and a faded clip that didn’t match the company’s new branding. When he saw Ellery looking, he tucked it back against his chest as if it were private.

‘Marcus has been with Mikey since before the company,’ Priya said lightly, as if it were trivia.

Marcus didn’t correct her. He didn’t confirm it either. He just watched the doors.

Product claimed the center of the room. Priya Shah moved easily between people, apologizing for the disruption even as she quietly made sure everyone was where they needed to be. She spoke in timelines and assurances, her phone never quite leaving her hand.

Science kept to the edges. Elias Varga watched the monitors instead of the introductions, correcting a data overlay under his breath as if SAM might take offense if left unchallenged. When he finally acknowledged Ellery, it was with a nod that suggested precision mattered more than courtesy.

A technician hovered near a cart of tools and diagnostic equipment. Jonah Pike’s hands moved constantly—adjusting a glove, straightening a cable that didn’t need it. He smiled when spoken to, the expression quick and gone.

Safety arrived last. Renée Calder did not rush. She did not apologize. She offered context instead, outlining regulatory expectations and prior concerns in a voice that suggested this conversation had been postponed, not unexpected.

Throughout it all, SAM spoke. Brief confirmations. Status updates. Acknowledgments delivered just quickly enough to be useful. Each time, Ellery felt the faintest sense of dissonance.

SAM sounded different depending on who addressed it. With Marcus, the tone flattened, procedural and clipped. With Priya, it was faster, almost anticipatory, as if meeting her halfway. When Elias spoke, the cadence tightened, precise and exacting, like a footnote read aloud.

Ellery clocked it and filed it. Later, alone in the interview room, she would write a single line in her notebook.

_Optimized for what?_

No one commented on the shifts. Which told her more than if they had.

As they walked, the layout of the facility made itself clear. Under normal conditions, only Elias Varga and Jonah Pike had direct access to the Charge Cage. Security controlled the doors. Product controlled the schedule. Safety controlled the paperwork. Michael ‘Mikey’ Holloway did not normally cross the line himself—he didn’t need to. Everyone controlled a piece of the system. No one controlled all of it.

‘This was a test run for a demonstration next week,’ Priya said, as if answering a question Ellery hadn’t asked yet. ‘Investors were coming. We needed confidence.’

Ellery looked around the room. At the people who had built their days, their careers, around this place. ‘And now?’ she asked.

No one answered right away.

Whatever had happened that morning, these people trusted the system. They trusted it the way people trusted infrastructure—roads, power lines, gravity itself. Not because it never failed, but because it usually didn’t.

Ellery wondered, not for the last time, what happened when usually stopped being enough.
