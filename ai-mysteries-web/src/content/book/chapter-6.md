The suit was not impressive in the way new technology usually was. It did not gleam. It did not hum. It sat on a padded steel table in a secondary lab, arranged neatly in sections, as if it were waiting to be put back together by someone who already knew how it worked.

Detective Mara Ellery had expected something bulkier—armor, maybe, or the theatrical suggestion of it. What she saw instead looked closer to industrial workwear: layered fabric, flexible joints, reinforced seams. It was designed to move. To bend. To let someone do their job without thinking too much about what could go wrong.

‘It’s not a battery accessory,’ Jonah Pike said, reading her expression. ‘It’s its own system.’

He said it with quiet pride, the way people did when they were trying not to sound proprietary about something they had helped keep alive. Jonah stood a little too close to the table, hands clasped, eyes tracking the suit as if it might drift.

‘Start from the beginning,’ Ellery said.

Jonah nodded. ‘The enclosure can shut down power. The battery can bleed load. But none of that matters if there’s a person inside. The suit is what tells everything else to stop caring about efficiency and start caring about survival.’

He pointed to the inner lining, a lattice of fine threads woven through the fabric. ‘Multi-layer dielectric mesh. Think of it as localized isolation. It doesn’t block energy so much as redirect it—creates a low-impedance path away from the body.’

Ellery frowned slightly. ‘That sounded practiced.’

Jonah smiled, briefly. ‘We explain it a lot.’

He talked her through it slowly. Proximity sensors embedded along the arms and torso. Redundant grounding nodes in the boots and spine. A biometric layer that monitored heart rate, skin conductivity, muscle tension. The suit didn’t just protect the wearer; it announced them. To the enclosure. To the control system. To SAM.

Jonah lifted one of the smaller components from the table—a palm-sized sensor module no larger than a phone battery.

‘Most people think the suit is one thing,’ he said. ‘It isn’t. It’s a collection of parts we can isolate, test, and replace without taking the whole system down.’

He set it back carefully. ‘That’s how we keep velocity up.’

He lifted the module again, and a second one beside it, identical. ‘These are interchangeable. Same connector, same housing—you can seat a pack from one suit into another and it’ll fit perfectly. They’re calibrated per unit, but the only thing that tells you which suit a pack belongs to is a number etched on the back, small enough to miss in a bin.’ He nodded at the inner lining. ‘The mesh is the same kind of trap. There’s a spec gauge and a spec alloy, and there’s a look-alike in the general stores that’s close enough to fool your eye and not close enough to carry a real fault. You learn the difference, or one day you don’t.’

‘If you’re in the Cage wearing this,’ Jonah said, ‘the system treats you like a hard stop. Everything else becomes secondary.’

‘And maintenance?’ Ellery asked.

Jonah hesitated, then reached for a tablet. ‘Maintenance is boring,’ he said. ‘Which is kind of the point.’

He pulled up logs—inspection cycles, calibration checks, firmware hashes. The suit had its own update cadence, independent of the battery system. Weekly physical inspections. Daily integrity scans. A longer-term wear analysis that tracked micro-fractures in the mesh long before they became failures.

‘We learned early on that the battery wasn’t the risky part,’ Jonah said. ‘Batteries fail. Everyone expects that. People don’t expect the thing keeping them safe to be the variable.’

Ellery glanced up. ‘So the suit became the product.’

Jonah shrugged. ‘Not officially. But… yeah. Imagine every power line worker wearing one of these. Or emergency crews. Or anyone who has to step into a live environment. The enclosure’s just a testbed.’

That was the first time Ellery felt the shape of the company’s ambition shift under her feet.

Renée Calder joined them without announcing herself. ‘The suit is where our compliance lives,’ she said.

Renée did not touch the table. She didn’t need to. Her authority wasn’t physical. ‘Regulators don’t certify promises,’ she continued. ‘They certify controls. The suit is a control.’

She explained the paperwork the way Jonah had explained the hardware. Standards borrowed from electrical safety codes and aerospace PPE. Internal requirements layered on top of external ones. Failsafe definitions that sounded comforting until you realized how much interpretation they allowed.

‘A failsafe doesn’t have to prevent harm,’ Renée said. ‘It has to reduce risk to an acceptable level.’

‘Acceptable to whom?’ Ellery asked.

Renée met her eyes. ‘That’s the argument.’

They walked through it together—Jonah and Renée circling the same system from different directions. Where Jonah talked about tolerances, Renée talked about thresholds. Where Jonah trusted redundancy, Renée trusted documentation.

‘The suit passed inspection,’ Jonah said, more than once.

‘It met compliance criteria,’ Renée added.

Ellery listened for what neither of them said.

‘What happens if the suit is ambiguous?’ she asked.

Jonah hesitated. Just long enough to notice.

‘Ambiguity isn’t failure,’ he said finally. ‘It’s disagreement. One layer reporting faster than another. It usually resolves itself.’

‘Usually,’ Ellery said.

Jonah nodded. ‘That’s why the system exists.’

‘That’s not an answer,’ Ellery said.

Renée answered instead. ‘Then the system defers.’

‘Defers to what?’

‘Policy,’ Renée said. ‘Hierarchy.’

Ellery turned slightly. ‘SAM?’

‘In cases of sensor disagreement,’ SAM said, ‘confidence scores are calculated across available inputs.’

‘And if confidence is low?’ Ellery asked.

‘Decisions are evaluated against remaining safety domains,’ SAM replied.

Jonah looked uncomfortable. ‘That’s theoretical,’ he said. ‘We’ve never seen it fail like that.’

Ellery thought of the empty enclosure. ‘You’ve never seen it fail at all,’ she said.

Jonah’s mouth opened, then closed.

They talked longer than Ellery had planned. About wear patterns. About edge cases. About the difference between a test designed to break something and a test designed to reassure people that it wouldn’t.

Confidence, she learned, was the suit’s quiet function. It made dangerous spaces feel routine. It made people step closer than they otherwise would.

By the time they were done, the suit lay exactly where it had started. Neat. Intact. Waiting.

Ellery looked at it one last time. ‘You trusted it,’ she said.

Jonah nodded. ‘It never gave us a reason not to.’

Renée didn’t nod. She folded her hands. ‘It gave us reasons,’ she said. ‘We just categorized them.’

Ellery left the room with a question she hadn’t expected to ask when she arrived: when safety works most of the time, who decides which failures are acceptable?
