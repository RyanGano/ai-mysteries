Detective Mara Ellery arrived at the facility early in the afternoon, when the morning’s crisis had already been contained and translated into procedures. The building rose out of the industrial park with the careful confidence of money—clean lines, too much glass, landscaping that tried a little too hard to look accidental.

She’d heard of the company, of course. Everyone had. A few funding rounds that made the business pages. Buzzwords that traveled well outside their original meaning. Energy storage, resilience, next-generation infrastructure. The kind of place people described as ‘important’ without ever quite saying what it did.

What she hadn’t heard was the word murder.

That mattered.

The call had come through as a death investigation. Not a homicide. Not yet. A man had died inside a testing facility under circumstances no one fully understood. The local department had done what it was trained to do: secure the site, separate the people involved, make sure there wasn’t an obvious threat still loose in the building. And then they’d called her.

Find the cause, the lieutenant had said. Figure out what broke.

What the lieutenant did not say, because he had no reason to know it, was that the case had not simply landed on her. Ellery had asked for it. She had seen the company’s name in the morning’s briefing—three lines, a death at a battery facility, cause undetermined—and had gone to his office and requested it before the rest of the room had finished its coffee. He had been glad to hand it off. The work was tedious and she was thorough, and no one thinks to question a detective who volunteers for the job nobody wants.

Ellery showed her badge at the gate and was waved through without ceremony. Security here was polite, efficient, and clearly unused to saying no. She noticed that too.

Inside, the facility smelled faintly of ozone and coffee. People spoke in low voices, clustered in doorways, their attention snapping briefly toward her and then away again. No one looked panicked. They looked unsettled—like people waiting for an explanation that hadn’t arrived yet.

‘Detective Ellery,’ a uniformed officer said, falling into step beside her. ‘We’ve got the site locked down. No signs of forced entry. No obvious criminal activity.’

‘And no clear accident,’ Ellery said.

The officer hesitated. ‘That’s the problem.’

She followed him down a corridor that widened into an observation gallery. Thick glass ran floor to ceiling, looking out over a large enclosed chamber marked with hazard striping and warning lights that were now dark. The Charge Cage, someone murmured behind her, as if the name itself carried weight.

A man had died in there. Michael ‘Mikey’ Holloway. Founder, leader, the one whose name appeared most often in articles about the company.

Ellery didn’t step closer right away. She watched the room first—the way people stood, the way they avoided looking directly at the glass. No one was crying. No one was angry. It felt less like a crime scene and more like the aftermath of a decision everyone had agreed to and no one wanted to own.

‘He was wearing protective equipment,’ the officer said. ‘The kind that’s supposed to prevent exactly this.’

‘Supposed to,’ Ellery repeated.

She’d learned a long time ago that deaths like this didn’t announce themselves as violence. They arrived wrapped in language: procedures, safeguards, assumptions. Everyone involved had done what they believed was reasonable. That didn’t mean someone hadn’t been careless. It didn’t mean something hadn’t been ignored. But it did mean the answer might not look like a person with a motive.

‘I want to be clear,’ she said, mostly to herself. ‘I’m not here to find a killer.’

The officer glanced at her.

‘I’m here to find out why a system people trusted did something they didn’t expect,’ Ellery finished.

Beyond the glass, the Charge Cage sat silent and inert. Whatever had happened, it wasn’t happening anymore.

That, Ellery knew, was often the most dangerous part.
