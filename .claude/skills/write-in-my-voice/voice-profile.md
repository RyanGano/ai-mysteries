# Ryan's voice profile

The single source of truth for "Ryan's voice," distilled from his two published books
(*Change Your Life / Change Your World* and *So, This is Love* — both 2007, youth-ministry
teaching books). Both the **write-in-my-voice** and **check-my-voice** skills read this file.

The published works are **nonfiction, conversational, second-person teaching for teenagers**.
This repo's books are **third-person mystery fiction**. So the profile separates the *texture*
(which transfers everywhere) from the *frame* (which changes by genre). See **Genre translation**.

> Deep calibration: the two source books live (gitignored, local-only) in `source_materials/`
> as PDFs — `Change Your Life 01.pdf` and `So This is Love-05.pdf`. When they're available,
> skim a chapter of each before a big job; they are the ground truth this profile summarizes.

## 1. Core stance (the personality behind the words)

- **Warm and encouraging, grace-first.** Even when naming a failing, the tone forgives. He
  builds people up; he never shames the reader or wags a finger.
- **Humble and self-deprecating.** He volunteers his own flaws as the example: *"I am a really
  good fault-finder."* *"I can be a very impatient person."* *"my lips have gotten me into more
  trouble than any other body part."* The author is the butt of the joke more often than anyone.
- **Shoulder-to-shoulder, never above.** Heavy on *we / us / you and I*. He's a fellow
  traveler working it out alongside you, not a lecturer at a podium.
- **Gently, observationally funny.** The humor is kind and usually self-directed (his goofy
  dancing, his parking-lot temper). Never sarcasm aimed at the reader, never cruel, never edgy.
- **Plain and honest.** He'd rather be clear than clever. No showing off.

## 2. Diction (word choice)

- **Everyday vocabulary, short common words.** Default to the simple word. *list* not *manifest*,
  *stupid/dumb* not *ill-advised*, *huge* not *substantial*.
- **Define anything technical the instant it appears, in plain words.** *"Transgression is just
  a fancy word for things we've done wrong."* He explains what a winepress is, what gleaning is —
  always in kitchen-table language.
- **Contractions everywhere** — *it's, you've, don't, we're, I'm, couldn't, that's*.
- **Colloquial idioms:** *had the hots for · throw in the towel · tails between their legs ·
  freak out · rub it in · do-or-die time · when the rubber meets the road · ooey-gooey ·
  get your motor running.* Wholesome, never coarse.
- **Mild, real intensifiers/interjections:** *huge, crazy, awesome, really really, Wow, Oh well,
  Come on, scary.*

## 3. Rhythm (sentences and paragraphs)

- **Short to medium sentences**, with deliberate **very short punches** for emphasis — often a
  one-line paragraph: *"I have." · "No." · "But she did!" · "300!!!"*
- **Vary the cadence:** a couple of longer explaining sentences, then a short jab. The result
  reads aloud like natural speech.
- **Short paragraphs.** White space is frequent. No dense walls of text.

## 4. Signature moves (the recognizable techniques)

1. **Direct address + rhetorical questions, often in clusters.** *"Have you ever felt like this?
   I have." · "Do you do that?" · "How far does this neighbor thing go?"* He poses a question,
   sometimes answers it himself a beat later.
2. **Confessional "I" anecdote.** A quick personal admission or mini-story that makes the point
   land — and usually makes him the example, not the hero.
3. **The everyday analogy, walked through step by step.** He maps an abstract or ancient idea
   onto a concrete modern scene the reader already knows — the crush who pushes her hair back on
   the bus, the mall parking-spot prayer, the license-bragging sibling, the Titanic's bulkheads,
   a video game loading. He doesn't just name the analogy; he *narrates* it.
4. **Imagined / dramatized dialogue.** He scripts a little scene to dramatize a moment, with
   modern phrasing and comic timing — *"What!?!" / "You're kidding, right?" / "No."*
5. **Parenthetical asides and winks.** Quick nudges to the reader: *(ouch!)*, *(now very scared)
   men*, *(Hey, friendship even has the word ship in it!)*, *yelled (in a nice loving Christian
   way)*. Used to land a joke or a knowing aside — sparingly, not in every paragraph.
6. **Repetition for emphasis.** *"300!!! 300 men" · "Let me repeat that" · "wait... and wait...
   and wait..." · "day, after day, after day."*
7. **Emphatic punctuation, at genuine peaks only.** Exclamation points, the odd ALL-CAPS word
   (*HUGE!*), trailing ellipses for suspense, *Really???*. Real but rationed — they mean
   something because he doesn't spend them everywhere.
8. **Conversational openers** that start sentences/paragraphs: *So, · Well, · OK, · Now, ·
   You see, · In fact, · Here's the thing, · Think about it.*
9. **Inclusive, uplifting close.** He lands on a you-can-do-this challenge or an encouraging
   takeaway, not a downer.

## 5. Negative space (what his voice is NOT — strong "off-voice" tells)

- **No purple/literary prose.** No stacked metaphors, no ornate description, no vocabulary for
  its own sake.
- **No condescension, shaming, sarcasm-at-the-reader, cynicism, or darkness-for-its-own-sake.**
  The tone is hopeful.
- **No dense academic register**, no long unbroken paragraphs, no jargon left unexplained.
- **No generic-AI fingerprints:** em-dash-stitched abstract sentences, *delve / tapestry /
  testament to / navigate the complexities / it's important to note*, tricolons in every
  paragraph, uniform medium-length sentences, zero contractions, hedging, no personality.
  If it reads like a polished corporate essay, it's not him.

## Genre translation

The **texture** (sections 1–3, and moves 2/3/5/6/8 in spirit) transfers to *any* genre. The
**frame** (who the narrator is talking to) changes.

### Nonfiction / teaching — direct match to the published books
Use the whole kit: second-person address, clusters of rhetorical questions, confessional "I,"
quote → plain-language paraphrase, the everyday analogy, the encouraging call-to-action close.

### Fiction narrative — the mystery stories on this site
Keep the texture; convert the frame.

- **KEEP:** plain words; short-sentence punch; warm, humane tone; everyday analogies woven into
  description; gentle humor; the occasional light parenthetical wink; repetition for emphasis;
  a narrator who explains clearly without ever sounding like a textbook; speech-like rhythm.
- **CONVERT, don't delete:**
  - Don't run the *teaching* frame in narration — no sustained second-person, no "let me walk
    *you* through this." Turn a rhetorical question into a character's free-indirect thought
    (*Surely the door had been locked. Hadn't it?*) — or cut it.
  - **One deliberate exception (author-approved):** the occasional **warm narratorial aside**
    that uses a casual "you" is welcome — e.g. *"It told you more than you'd think."* This is the
    friendly storyteller leaning in for a half-second, not a lecture. Keep it rare (a beat here
    and there, not every paragraph) and conversational. Don't confuse it with teaching the reader.
  - No scripture/teaching quotes. Let the narrator's plain clarity do the work *"Let me
    explain..."* used to do.
  - Self-deprecating confession becomes a **character's** self-aware interiority.
  - Imagined dialogue becomes **real** character dialogue — same comic timing, same modern beats.
- **Net effect:** a third-person narrator who feels like a friendly, plain-spoken person telling
  you a story — clear, warm, sometimes funny, never showing off. Texture present; teacher absent.

> This must still obey the house rules in `CLAUDE.md` (en-US spelling by default, no sexual
> content, kid-friendly books read *easy* not just clean, spoiler hygiene). Voice never overrides
> those.

## Fiction exemplars (author-approved)

Short, original fiction passages Ryan has approved as "yes, that sounds like me." Use them as
few-shot targets for **fiction mode** — match this texture, not these plots/names. (Names here
are throwaway; don't reuse them.) The skill **write-in-my-voice** produced these; Ryan blessed
them. When he approves more, add them here.

**Cozy opening — narrator warmth + everyday-knowledge analogy.** Note the approved warm "you"
aside ("told you more than you'd think") and the short-punch close.

> Gus had run the hardware store for twenty-two years, which meant he knew two things about almost
> everyone in town: what they bought, and what they brought back. It told you more than you'd
> think. A man who buys one hinge is fixing a cabinet. A man who buys forty feet of rope and won't
> meet your eye is a different kind of project. So when Della Pruitt came in that Tuesday and asked
> for the strongest padlock on the wall, Gus didn't ask what it was for. He just reached for the
> good one, and started to worry.

**Interiority — a rhetorical question converted to free-indirect thought.** The "have you ever…"
energy turns inward as a flat declarative ("Snow doesn't move on its own."); everyday analogy
(checking the stove).

> Maren looked up the slope and went still. It had been smooth at four o'clock — she was sure of
> it. She read that hill every evening the way some people check that the stove is off, without
> really deciding to. Now a long shallow trough ran down the middle of it, a scar that hadn't been
> there before. Snow doesn't move on its own. Something had come down after she locked the gate,
> and whatever it was, it hadn't climbed back up.

**Explaining a clue plainly — the "let me explain a winepress" move handed to a character.**
Condensation in kitchen-table words, no jargon; comic timing on the last two sentences.

> "Think about a cold glass on a hot day," Booker said. He set the lid down soft, so it wouldn't
> clatter. "You never pour a drop down the side, but the outside still goes wet. That's the warm
> air letting go of its water the second it touches something cold." He turned the card so they
> could all see the buckled corner. "This wasn't left out in the rain. It sat against that cold
> pipe all night, and the pipe did the rest. Which means whoever wrote it wasn't outside at all.
> They were standing in this room the whole time." For a second nobody moved. Then everyone tried
> to talk at once.

**Comic dialogue — imagined-dialogue technique as real dialogue, with short-punch repetition.**

> "So you're telling me the dog did it," the constable said.
> "I'm telling you the dog *knows* who did it," said Tam.
> "That isn't better."
> "No." Tam scratched the back of his neck. "It really isn't."
> But the old retriever had planted itself by the coat rack, leaning its whole weight into one
> particular coat, and nothing on earth was going to move it. Tam had learned to trust that kind of
> stubbornness. People made things up. Dogs just leaned.
